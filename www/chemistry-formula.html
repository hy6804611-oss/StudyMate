📂 Atomic Structure

Number of Protons, Neutrons and Electrons

Mass Number (A) = Protons + Neutrons

📖 Meaning: The mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

🎯 When to Use: Used to find the number of particles inside an atom.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Neutrons = Mass Number − Atomic Number
  • Protons = Atomic Number
  • Electrons = Atomic Number (for neutral atoms)

💡 Memory Tip: Mass number = Heavy particles (protons + neutrons).

⚠️ Common Mistake: Electrons are not included in the mass number.

Relative Atomic Mass

Aᵣ = Σ(Isotope Mass × Abundance) ÷ Total Abundance

📖 Meaning: The average mass of an element based on the masses and abundance of its isotopes.

🎯 When to Use: Used when an element has different isotopes.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Average Atomic Mass = Weighted Average of Isotopes

💡 Memory Tip: More abundant isotopes contribute more to the average.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not calculate a simple average unless abundances are equal.

Number of Particles (Moles Formula)

N = n × Nₐ

📖 Meaning: Converts the number of moles into the number of particles.

🎯 When to Use: Used to find atoms, molecules or ions.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Number of Particles = Moles × Avogadro's Constant
  • n = N ÷ Nₐ

💡 SI Unit: Particles (atoms, molecules, ions)

⚠️ Common Mistake: Avogadro's constant = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Energy of an Electron

E = hf

📖 Meaning: The energy of electromagnetic radiation absorbed or released by electrons.

🎯 When to Use: Used in atomic transitions and electron energy calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Energy = Planck's Constant × Frequency
  • Frequency = Energy ÷ h

💡 Memory Tip: Higher frequency means higher energy.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Frequency must be measured in hertz (Hz).

📂 Mole Concept and Stoichiometry

Moles from Mass

n = m / M

📖 Meaning: The number of moles is the mass of a substance divided by its molar mass.

🎯 When to Use: Used to convert between mass and amount of substance.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Moles = Mass ÷ Molar Mass
  • Mass = Moles × Molar Mass
  • Molar Mass = Mass ÷ Moles

💡 SI Unit: Mole (mol)

⚠️ Common Mistake: Molar mass must be in g/mol.

Moles from Number of Particles

n = N / Nₐ

📖 Meaning: Calculates the number of moles from the number of atoms, molecules or ions.

🎯 When to Use: Used when the number of particles is given.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Number of Particles = Moles × Avogadro's Constant
  • N = nNₐ

💡 Memory Tip: Divide by Avogadro to go from particles to moles.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Molar Concentration

C = n / V

📖 Meaning: Concentration is the amount of solute in a given volume of solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used in solution calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Concentration = Moles ÷ Volume
  • Moles = Concentration × Volume
  • Volume = Moles ÷ Concentration

💡 SI Unit: mol/dm³

⚠️ Common Mistake: Convert volume to dm³ before calculation.

Percentage Yield

% Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100

📖 Meaning: Measures how much product is obtained compared with the maximum possible amount.

🎯 When to Use: Used in chemical reaction efficiency calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Actual Yield = (% Yield × Theoretical Yield) ÷ 100

💡 Memory Tip: Actual is what you get; theoretical is what you should get.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Percentage yield cannot be greater than 100% in normal conditions.

Percentage Purity

% Purity = (Pure Substance Mass / Impure Sample Mass) × 100

📖 Meaning: Shows the percentage of a sample that is the desired pure substance.

🎯 When to Use: Used in chemical analysis.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Pure Mass = (% Purity × Total Mass) ÷ 100

💡 Memory Tip: Purity compares pure part to the whole sample.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not use total sample mass as pure mass.

📂 Gas Laws

Boyle's Law

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂

📖 Meaning: For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.

🎯 When to Use: Used when pressure and volume change while temperature remains constant.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • P₁ = P₂V₂ ÷ V₁
  • V₁ = P₂V₂ ÷ P₁
  • P₂ = P₁V₁ ÷ V₂

💡 Memory Tip: Pressure increases when volume decreases.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Temperature must remain constant.

Charles' Law

V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂

📖 Meaning: The volume of a gas is directly proportional to its temperature at constant pressure.

🎯 When to Use: Used when volume changes with temperature.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • V₂ = V₁T₂ ÷ T₁
  • T₂ = V₂T₁ ÷ V₁

💡 Memory Tip: Higher temperature gives higher volume.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Always use Kelvin temperature.

Pressure Law

P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂

📖 Meaning: The pressure of a gas is directly proportional to temperature at constant volume.

🎯 When to Use: Used when pressure changes with temperature.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • P₂ = P₁T₂ ÷ T₁
  • T₂ = P₂T₁ ÷ P₁

💡 Memory Tip: Heating a gas in a fixed container increases pressure.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not use Celsius in gas law calculations.

Combined Gas Law

P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂

📖 Meaning: Combines pressure, volume and temperature relationships of a gas.

🎯 When to Use: Used when pressure, volume and temperature all change.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • P₁V₁T₂ = P₂V₂T₁

💡 Memory Tip: Pressure and volume stay together; temperature stays below.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Convert all temperatures to Kelvin.

Ideal Gas Equation

PV = nRT

📖 Meaning: Relates pressure, volume, temperature and amount of gas.

🎯 When to Use: Used for ideal gas calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • P = nRT ÷ V
  • V = nRT ÷ P
  • n = PV ÷ RT
  • T = PV ÷ nR

💡 SI Unit: Pressure (Pa), Volume (m³), Temperature (K)

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use the correct gas constant R.

📂 States of Matter

Density of a Substance

ρ = m / V

📖 Meaning: Density is the mass contained in a unit volume of a substance.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate density, mass or volume of solids, liquids and gases.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Density = Mass ÷ Volume
  • Mass = Density × Volume
  • Volume = Mass ÷ Density

💡 SI Unit: kg/m³

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse density with weight.

Pressure in a Liquid

P = ρgh

📖 Meaning: Liquid pressure depends on density, gravitational field strength and depth.

🎯 When to Use: Used for pressure calculations in liquids.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Pressure = Density × Gravity × Depth
  • Depth = P ÷ ρg
  • Density = P ÷ gh

💡 Memory Tip: Deeper liquids have higher pressure.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Depth must be measured vertically.

Boyle's Relationship

P ∝ 1/V

📖 Meaning: For a fixed mass of gas, pressure increases as volume decreases.

🎯 When to Use: Used to explain gas compression.

📝 Formula Relationship:

  • High Pressure → Low Volume
  • Low Pressure → High Volume

💡 Memory Tip: Pressure and volume move in opposite directions.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Temperature must remain constant.

Charles' Relationship

V ∝ T

📖 Meaning: The volume of a gas increases when its temperature increases at constant pressure.

🎯 When to Use: Used to describe gas expansion.

📝 Formula Relationship:

  • Higher Temperature → Higher Volume
  • Lower Temperature → Lower Volume

💡 Memory Tip: Hot gases expand.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Temperature must be in Kelvin.

Kinetic Energy of Gas Molecules

Average KE ∝ T

📖 Meaning: The average kinetic energy of gas molecules increases with temperature.

🎯 When to Use: Used to explain molecular motion in gases.

💡 Memory Tip: Higher temperature means faster molecular movement.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Temperature affects particle speed, not particle size.

📂 Periodic Table and Periodicity

Atomic Number

Z = Number of Protons

📖 Meaning: Atomic number is the number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom.

🎯 When to Use: Used to identify an element and determine electron arrangement.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Protons = Atomic Number
  • Electrons = Atomic Number (for neutral atoms)

💡 Memory Tip: Atomic number identifies the element.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Atomic number is not the same as mass number.

Mass Number

A = Z + N

📖 Meaning: Mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons in an atom.

🎯 When to Use: Used to find the number of neutrons.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Neutrons = Mass Number − Atomic Number
  • Protons = Mass Number − Neutrons

💡 Memory Tip: Mass comes from nucleus particles.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Electrons are not included in mass number.

Effective Nuclear Charge

Zₑff = Z − S

📖 Meaning: Effective nuclear charge is the net positive charge experienced by an electron after shielding.

🎯 When to Use: Used to explain atomic size and periodic trends.

📝 Formula:

  • Z = Nuclear Charge
  • S = Shielding Effect

💡 Memory Tip: More shielding reduces attraction.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse nuclear charge with effective nuclear charge.

Electronegativity Difference

ΔEN = EN₁ − EN₂

📖 Meaning: Difference in electronegativity helps predict the type of chemical bond formed.

🎯 When to Use: Used to determine bond polarity.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Large difference → More ionic character
  • Small difference → More covalent character

💡 Memory Tip: Bigger electronegativity difference means stronger attraction difference.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Always subtract the smaller value from the larger value.

Ion Charge

Charge = Protons − Electrons

📖 Meaning: The charge of an ion depends on the difference between protons and electrons.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate positive or negative ion charge.

📝 Formula:

  • More protons → Positive ion
  • More electrons → Negative ion

💡 Memory Tip: Proton = Positive, Electron = Negative.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not count neutrons because they have no charge.

📂 Chemical Bonding

Lattice Energy

ΔHₗₐₜ ∝ (q₁q₂) / r

📖 Meaning: Lattice energy is the energy released when oppositely charged ions form an ionic lattice.

🎯 When to Use: Used to compare strength of ionic bonds.

📝 Formula Relationship:

  • Higher ion charge → Higher lattice energy
  • Smaller ion size → Higher lattice energy

💡 Memory Tip: Stronger attraction means more energy.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse lattice energy with bond energy.

Bond Energy

Energy Change = Energy of Bonds Broken − Energy of Bonds Formed

📖 Meaning: Calculates the energy change during a chemical reaction.

🎯 When to Use: Used in bond enthalpy calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Positive value → Endothermic reaction
  • Negative value → Exothermic reaction

💡 Memory Tip: Breaking bonds needs energy; forming bonds releases energy.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not reverse broken and formed bonds.

Dipole Moment

μ = q × d

📖 Meaning: Dipole moment measures the separation of charges in a polar bond.

🎯 When to Use: Used to determine polarity of molecules.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Dipole Moment = Charge × Distance
  • q = μ ÷ d
  • d = μ ÷ q

💡 SI Unit: Coulomb metre (C m)

⚠️ Common Mistake: A molecule can have polar bonds but be non-polar overall.

Formal Charge

FC = V − (N + B/2)

📖 Meaning: Formal charge shows the charge distribution of atoms in a molecule.

🎯 When to Use: Used in Lewis structure calculations.

📝 Formula:

  • V = Valence Electrons
  • N = Non-bonding Electrons
  • B = Bonding Electrons

💡 Memory Tip: Formal charge helps choose the most stable structure.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Count bonding electrons correctly.

Bond Order

Bond Order = Number of Bonds Between Two Atoms

📖 Meaning: Bond order shows the number and strength of bonds between atoms.

🎯 When to Use: Used to compare bond strength and length.

📝 Relationship:

  • Higher bond order → Stronger bond
  • Higher bond order → Shorter bond length

💡 Memory Tip: More bonds mean stronger attraction.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse bond order with number of atoms.

📂 Acids, Bases and Salts

pH Formula

pH = -log[H⁺]

📖 Meaning: pH measures the acidity of a solution based on hydrogen ion concentration.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate acidity of acids.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ
  • Lower pH = Higher hydrogen ion concentration

💡 Memory Tip: pH below 7 is acidic, pH 7 is neutral, pH above 7 is basic.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not forget the negative sign.

pOH Formula

pOH = -log[OH⁻]

📖 Meaning: pOH measures the concentration of hydroxide ions in a solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used for alkaline solutions.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • [OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴ

💡 Memory Tip: pH and pOH describe opposite ions.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse pH with pOH.

Relationship Between pH and pOH

pH + pOH = 14

📖 Meaning: Shows the relationship between hydrogen ion and hydroxide ion concentration at 25°C.

🎯 When to Use: Used when one value is known and the other is required.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • pH = 14 − pOH
  • pOH = 14 − pH

💡 Memory Tip: The total is always 14 at 25°C.

⚠️ Common Mistake: This value changes with temperature.

Acid Dissociation Constant (Ka)

Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]

📖 Meaning: Measures the strength of an acid in solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used to compare strong and weak acids.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Larger Ka = Stronger acid
  • Smaller Ka = Weaker acid

💡 Memory Tip: Strong acids release more hydrogen ions.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse Ka with concentration.

Base Dissociation Constant (Kb)

Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻] / [B]

📖 Meaning: Measures the strength of a base in solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used to compare weak bases.

📝 Formula:

  • Larger Kb = Stronger base
  • Smaller Kb = Weaker base

💡 Memory Tip: Kb is for bases; Ka is for acids.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not mix acid and base constants.

📂 Redox Reactions

Oxidation Number

Oxidation Number = Charge Assigned to an Atom

📖 Meaning: Oxidation number shows the apparent charge of an atom in a compound.

🎯 When to Use: Used to identify oxidation and reduction reactions.

📝 Common Rules:

  • Free elements = 0
  • Monatomic ions = their ionic charge
  • Oxygen usually = −2
  • Hydrogen usually = +1

💡 Memory Tip: Oxidation is increase in oxidation number.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse oxidation number with valency.

Oxidation State Change

Electrons Lost = Electrons Gained

📖 Meaning: In redox reactions, electrons lost by one substance equal electrons gained by another.

🎯 When to Use: Used to balance redox equations.

📝 Formula Relationship:

  • Oxidation = Loss of Electrons
  • Reduction = Gain of Electrons

💡 Memory Tip: OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not reverse oxidation and reduction.

Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis

m = ZIt

📖 Meaning: The mass of substance deposited during electrolysis depends on current and time.

🎯 When to Use: Used in electrolysis calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Mass = Electrochemical Equivalent × Current × Time
  • Z = m ÷ It
  • I = m ÷ Zt

💡 SI Unit: kg

⚠️ Common Mistake: Time must be in seconds.

Charge Passed During Electrolysis

Q = It

📖 Meaning: The total charge passing through an electrolyte equals current multiplied by time.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate charge in electrochemical processes.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Charge = Current × Time
  • Current = Charge ÷ Time
  • Time = Charge ÷ Current

💡 SI Unit: Coulomb (C)

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use seconds, not minutes or hours.

Number of Electrons Transferred

Q = ne

📖 Meaning: Charge is equal to the number of electrons multiplied by electron charge.

🎯 When to Use: Used in redox and electrochemical calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Number of Electrons = Charge ÷ Electron Charge
  • n = Q ÷ e

💡 Memory Tip: One electron has charge 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse n (electrons) with moles.

📂 Electrochemistry

Electrode Potential

Ecell = Ecathode − Eanode

📖 Meaning: The cell potential is the difference between the reduction potentials of the cathode and anode.

🎯 When to Use: Used to determine the voltage of an electrochemical cell.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Cell Potential = Cathode Potential − Anode Potential
  • Ecathode = Ecell + Eanode
  • Eanode = Ecathode − Ecell

💡 Memory Tip: Cathode is always written first.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not subtract in the wrong order.

Nernst Equation

E = E° − (0.0592/n) log Q

📖 Meaning: Shows how electrode potential changes with concentration of ions.

🎯 When to Use: Used for non-standard electrochemical conditions.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • E = Cell potential
  • E° = Standard cell potential
  • n = Number of electrons transferred
  • Q = Reaction quotient

💡 Memory Tip: Concentration affects cell voltage.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use logarithm correctly.

Electrolysis Charge Formula

Q = It

📖 Meaning: The amount of electrical charge passed through an electrolyte.

🎯 When to Use: Used in electrolysis calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Charge = Current × Time
  • I = Q ÷ t
  • t = Q ÷ I

💡 SI Unit: Coulomb (C)

⚠️ Common Mistake: Convert time to seconds.

Electrochemical Equivalent

Z = m / It

📖 Meaning: The mass deposited per unit charge passed through an electrolyte.

🎯 When to Use: Used to find deposited mass during electrolysis.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Mass = ZIt
  • Current = m ÷ Zt
  • Time = m ÷ ZI

💡 Memory Tip: More current and time produce more deposited material.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Keep units consistent.

Faraday Constant Relationship

Q = nF

📖 Meaning: Relates electrical charge to the amount of electrons transferred.

🎯 When to Use: Used in mole calculations during electrolysis.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Number of Moles of Electrons = Charge ÷ Faraday Constant
  • n = Q ÷ F

💡 SI Unit: Coulomb per mole (C/mol)

⚠️ Common Mistake: Faraday constant ≈ 96500 C/mol.

📂 Chemical Equilibrium

Equilibrium Constant (Kc)

Kc = [Products]ᶜᵒᵉᶠᶠⁱᶜⁱᵉⁿᵗˢ / [Reactants]ᶜᵒᵉᶠᶠⁱᶜⁱᵉⁿᵗˢ

📖 Meaning: Kc shows the ratio of product concentrations to reactant concentrations at equilibrium.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate the position of chemical equilibrium.

📝 Formula Example:

  • For: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD
  • Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ ÷ [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ

💡 Memory Tip: Products are on top, reactants are below.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not include pure solids and liquids in Kc expressions.

Equilibrium Constant (Kp)

Kp = (Partial Pressure of Products) / (Partial Pressure of Reactants)

📖 Meaning: Kp uses gas pressures instead of concentrations.

🎯 When to Use: Used for gaseous equilibrium reactions.

📝 Formula Example:

  • Kp = (PᶜC × PᵈD) ÷ (PᵃA × PᵇB)

💡 Memory Tip: Kc uses concentration, Kp uses pressure.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Only gases are included in Kp.

Relationship Between Kp and Kc

Kp = Kc(RT)ⁿ

📖 Meaning: Relates equilibrium constants based on pressure and concentration.

🎯 When to Use: Used when converting between Kp and Kc.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • R = Gas constant
  • T = Temperature in Kelvin
  • n = Change in number of gas moles

💡 Memory Tip: n = Products' gas moles − Reactants' gas moles.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use Kelvin temperature.

Le Chatelier's Principle

Stress → Equilibrium Shifts

📖 Meaning: A system at equilibrium moves to reduce any applied change.

🎯 When to Use: Used to predict effects of concentration, pressure and temperature changes.

📝 Rules:

  • Increase reactant → shifts to products
  • Increase pressure → shifts to side with fewer gas moles
  • Increase temperature → shifts in endothermic direction

💡 Memory Tip: The system tries to oppose the change.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Catalysts do not change equilibrium position.

Reaction Quotient (Q)

Q = [Products] / [Reactants]

📖 Meaning: Compares the current state of a reaction with equilibrium.

🎯 When to Use: Used to predict the direction of reaction movement.

📝 Relationship:

  • Q < K → Reaction moves forward
  • Q > K → Reaction moves backward
  • Q = K → System is at equilibrium

💡 Memory Tip: Compare Q with K to know direction.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse Q with K.

📂 Rates of Reaction (Kinetics)

Rate of Reaction

Rate = Change in Concentration / Time

📖 Meaning: The rate of reaction measures how quickly reactants are converted into products.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate reaction speed.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Rate = ΔConcentration ÷ ΔTime
  • Change in Concentration = Rate × Time
  • Time = Change in Concentration ÷ Rate

💡 SI Unit: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

⚠️ Common Mistake: Time must be measured in seconds for SI units.

Rate Constant Equation

Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ

📖 Meaning: Shows how concentration affects the rate of a reaction.

🎯 When to Use: Used in rate law calculations.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • k = Rate constant
  • [A], [B] = Reactant concentrations
  • m, n = Reaction orders

💡 Memory Tip: Orders show how much each reactant affects rate.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Reaction order must come from experimental data.

First Order Half-Life

t½ = 0.693 / k

📖 Meaning: The half-life of a first-order reaction depends only on the rate constant.

🎯 When to Use: Used for first-order radioactive and chemical reactions.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • k = 0.693 ÷ t½

💡 Memory Tip: Larger k means faster reaction.

⚠️ Common Mistake: This formula applies only to first-order reactions.

Arrhenius Equation

k = Ae⁻ᴱᵃ⁄ᴿᵀ

📖 Meaning: Shows how temperature affects the rate constant of a reaction.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate activation energy and temperature effects.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • A = Frequency factor
  • Eₐ = Activation energy
  • R = Gas constant
  • T = Temperature in Kelvin

💡 Memory Tip: Higher temperature increases reaction rate.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Temperature must be in Kelvin.

Collision Energy Condition

Energy of Collision ≥ Activation Energy

📖 Meaning: Particles must collide with enough energy to react.

🎯 When to Use: Used to explain factors affecting reaction rates.

📝 Factors Increasing Rate:

  • Increase temperature
  • Increase concentration
  • Increase surface area
  • Add catalyst

💡 Memory Tip: More successful collisions = faster reaction.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Not every collision produces a reaction.

📂 Energetics (Thermochemistry)

Heat Energy

Q = mcΔT

📖 Meaning: Calculates the heat energy gained or lost by a substance.

🎯 When to Use: Used in temperature change and calorimetry calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Heat Energy = Mass × Specific Heat Capacity × Temperature Change
  • m = Q ÷ cΔT
  • c = Q ÷ mΔT
  • ΔT = Q ÷ mc

💡 SI Unit: Joule (J)

⚠️ Common Mistake: Temperature change is final temperature minus initial temperature.

Enthalpy Change

ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)

📖 Meaning: Measures the heat change during a chemical reaction at constant pressure.

🎯 When to Use: Used to determine whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • Negative ΔH = Exothermic reaction
  • Positive ΔH = Endothermic reaction

💡 Memory Tip: Products lower than reactants means energy is released.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not change the sign of ΔH incorrectly.

Enthalpy Change Using Heat

ΔH = -Q / n

📖 Meaning: Calculates molar enthalpy change from heat energy and moles.

🎯 When to Use: Used in calorimetry experiments.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Heat Energy = -ΔH × n
  • Moles = -Q ÷ ΔH

💡 SI Unit: kJ mol⁻¹

⚠️ Common Mistake: Convert joules to kilojoules when needed.

Hess's Law

ΔHreaction = ΣΔHproducts − ΣΔHreactants

📖 Meaning: The total enthalpy change is independent of the reaction pathway.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate unknown enthalpy changes.

📝 Formula:

  • Add individual reaction enthalpies to find overall ΔH.

💡 Memory Tip: Energy change depends only on start and finish.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Reverse reactions change the sign of ΔH.

Bond Enthalpy Calculation

ΔH = Σ(Bonds Broken) − Σ(Bonds Formed)

📖 Meaning: Calculates reaction enthalpy using bond energies.

🎯 When to Use: Used when bond energies are provided.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Breaking bonds requires energy.
  • Forming bonds releases energy.

💡 Memory Tip: Broken minus formed.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not subtract in the opposite order.

📂 Solutions and Solubility

Molar Concentration

C = n / V

📖 Meaning: Molar concentration is the amount of solute present in a given volume of solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate concentration, moles or volume of a solution.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Concentration = Moles ÷ Volume
  • Moles = Concentration × Volume
  • Volume = Moles ÷ Concentration

💡 SI Unit: mol dm⁻³

⚠️ Common Mistake: Volume must be converted to dm³.

Dilution Formula

C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

📖 Meaning: Relates concentration and volume before and after dilution.

🎯 When to Use: Used when a solution is diluted by adding solvent.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • C₁ = C₂V₂ ÷ V₁
  • V₁ = C₂V₂ ÷ C₁
  • C₂ = C₁V₁ ÷ V₂

💡 Memory Tip: Dilution reduces concentration but keeps moles constant.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not change units between the two volumes.

Solubility

Solubility = Mass of Solute / Mass of Solvent × 100

📖 Meaning: Shows the amount of solute that dissolves in a solvent.

🎯 When to Use: Used to compare how soluble substances are.

📝 Formula:

  • Solubility is usually expressed per 100 g of solvent.

💡 Memory Tip: Higher solubility means more solute dissolves.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not use solution mass instead of solvent mass.

Percentage Concentration by Mass

% Mass = (Mass of Solute / Mass of Solution) × 100

📖 Meaning: Shows the percentage of solute in a solution by mass.

🎯 When to Use: Used for concentration expressed as percentage.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Mass of Solution = Solute Mass + Solvent Mass

💡 Memory Tip: Percentage compares part to whole.

⚠️ Common Mistake: The denominator is total solution mass.

Percentage Concentration by Volume

% Volume = (Volume of Solute / Volume of Solution) × 100

📖 Meaning: Shows the amount of liquid solute in a solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used mainly for liquid mixtures.

📝 Formula:

  • Volume of Solution = Solute Volume + Other Components

💡 Memory Tip: Use volume percentage for liquid-liquid solutions.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not use mass values in volume percentage.

📂 Organic Chemistry Basics

General Formula of Alkanes

CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

📖 Meaning: Represents the general formula for saturated hydrocarbons containing only single bonds.

🎯 When to Use: Used to find the molecular formula of an alkane.

📝 Formula Example:

  • n = number of carbon atoms
  • For n = 3 → C₃H₈ (Propane)

💡 Memory Tip: Alkane = 2n + 2 hydrogen atoms.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not use this formula for alkenes or alkynes.

General Formula of Alkenes

CₙH₂ₙ

📖 Meaning: Represents hydrocarbons containing one carbon-carbon double bond.

🎯 When to Use: Used to find molecular formula of alkenes.

📝 Formula Example:

  • n = number of carbon atoms
  • For n = 4 → C₄H₈ (Butene)

💡 Memory Tip: Alkene has two fewer hydrogens than alkane.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse alkene with alkane formula.

General Formula of Alkynes

CₙH₂ₙ₋₂

📖 Meaning: Represents hydrocarbons containing a carbon-carbon triple bond.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate molecular formula of alkynes.

📝 Formula Example:

  • n = number of carbon atoms
  • For n = 3 → C₃H₄ (Propyne)

💡 Memory Tip: Triple bond removes more hydrogen atoms.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not apply to saturated hydrocarbons.

Degree of Unsaturation

DBE = (2C + 2 + N − H − X) / 2

📖 Meaning: Measures the number of rings and multiple bonds in an organic compound.

🎯 When to Use: Used to determine structure of organic molecules.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • C = Carbon atoms
  • H = Hydrogen atoms
  • N = Nitrogen atoms
  • X = Halogen atoms

💡 Memory Tip: Double bond = 1 DBE, Ring = 1 DBE.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Halogens count like hydrogen atoms.

Molecular Formula from Empirical Formula

Molecular Formula = Empirical Formula × n

📖 Meaning: Converts the simplest ratio formula into the actual molecular formula.

🎯 When to Use: Used when empirical formula and molar mass are known.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • n = Molecular Mass ÷ Empirical Formula Mass

💡 Memory Tip: Multiply every atom in the empirical formula by n.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Calculate n before multiplying.

Alkene General Formula

CₙH₂ₙ

📖 Meaning: Shows the formula of hydrocarbons containing a carbon-carbon double bond.

  • Propene: n = 3 → C₃H₆

💡 Memory Tip: One double bond reduces hydrogen by two.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse CₙH₂ₙ with alkane formula.

Alkyne General Formula

CₙH₂ₙ₋₂

📖 Meaning: Represents hydrocarbons containing a carbon-carbon triple bond.

🎯 When to Use: Used for alkynes.

📝 Formula Example:

  • Ethyne: n = 2 → C₂H₂
  • Propyne: n = 3 → C₃H₄

💡 Memory Tip: Triple bonds remove more hydrogen.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not apply to alkenes.

Combustion of Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbon + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

📖 Meaning: Complete combustion produces carbon dioxide and water.

🎯 When to Use: Used in combustion reaction calculations.

📝 Balancing Shortcut:

  • Balance carbon first.
  • Balance hydrogen second.
  • Balance oxygen last.

💡 Memory Tip: Complete combustion always gives CO₂ and H₂O.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Incomplete combustion may produce carbon monoxide or carbon.

Heat of Combustion

ΔHc = Heat Released ÷ Moles Burned

📖 Meaning: The energy released when one mole of a substance burns completely.

🎯 When to Use: Used in fuel energy calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Heat Released = ΔHc × Moles

💡 Memory Tip: Combustion enthalpy is usually negative because heat is released.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not forget the negative sign for energy released.

📂 Isomerism

Degree of Unsaturation (DBE)

DBE = (2C + 2 + N − H − X) / 2

📖 Meaning: Measures the number of rings and multiple bonds present in an organic compound.

🎯 When to Use: Used to determine possible structures of organic molecules.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • C = Number of carbon atoms
  • H = Number of hydrogen atoms
  • N = Number of nitrogen atoms
  • X = Number of halogen atoms

💡 Memory Tip: One ring or one double bond = 1 degree of unsaturation.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Oxygen atoms are ignored in DBE calculations.

Cis-Trans Isomer Condition

C=C + Different Groups on Each Carbon → Cis/Trans Isomers

📖 Meaning: Cis-trans isomerism occurs when groups are arranged differently around a double bond.

🎯 When to Use: Used to identify geometrical isomers.

📝 Formula Rule:

  • Cis = Similar groups on the same side
  • Trans = Similar groups on opposite sides

💡 Memory Tip: Double bonds restrict rotation.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Free rotation is possible in single bonds.

Optical Activity

Chiral Carbon = Carbon Attached to 4 Different Groups

📖 Meaning: A chiral carbon creates molecules that can exist as mirror-image isomers.

🎯 When to Use: Used to identify optical isomers.

📝 Formula Rule:

  • One chiral centre can produce two enantiomers.

💡 Memory Tip: Four different attachments create chirality.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Not every carbon atom is chiral.

Number of Possible Stereoisomers

Number of Isomers = 2ⁿ

📖 Meaning: Estimates the maximum number of stereoisomers based on chiral centres.

🎯 When to Use: Used for molecules with multiple chiral centres.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • n = Number of chiral centres

💡 Memory Tip: More chiral centres give more possible arrangements.

⚠️ Common Mistake: The formula gives maximum possible isomers; actual number may be lower.

📂 Polymers

Degree of Polymerization

n = Molecular Mass of Polymer / Molecular Mass of Monomer

📖 Meaning: Degree of polymerization is the number of repeating units present in a polymer chain.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate the number of monomer units in a polymer.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Number of Units = Polymer Mass ÷ Monomer Mass
  • Monomer Mass = Polymer Mass ÷ n

💡 Memory Tip: Larger n means a longer polymer chain.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse degree of polymerization with molecular mass.

Addition Polymerization

n(CH₂=CHR) → (−CH₂−CHR−)ₙ

📖 Meaning: Small unsaturated monomers join together without producing a by-product.

🎯 When to Use: Used for polymers formed from alkenes.

📝 Examples:

  • Ethene → Polyethene
  • Propene → Polypropene

💡 Memory Tip: Double bond opens to form a chain.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Addition polymerization does not remove small molecules.

Condensation Polymerization

Monomers → Polymer + Small Molecule

📖 Meaning: Monomers join together while releasing small molecules such as water.

🎯 When to Use: Used for polyesters and polyamides.

📝 Formula Relationship:

  • Polymer = Monomers − Small Molecule Loss

💡 Memory Tip: Condensation means something is removed.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse condensation with addition polymerization.

Percentage Yield of Polymer

% Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100

📖 Meaning: Measures the efficiency of a polymer production process.

🎯 When to Use: Used in industrial chemistry calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Actual Yield = (% Yield × Theoretical Yield) ÷ 100

💡 Memory Tip: Actual yield is what you obtain in reality.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Actual yield cannot be greater than theoretical yield.

Repeat Unit Mass

Polymer Mass = Number of Repeat Units × Repeat Unit Mass

📖 Meaning: Relates polymer mass to its repeating structure.

🎯 When to Use: Used when calculating polymer chain properties.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Repeat Unit Mass = Polymer Mass ÷ Number of Units

💡 Memory Tip: A polymer is many repeating units joined together.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use the mass of the repeating unit, not the whole monomer chain.

📂 Metals and Extraction

Percentage Composition of an Element

% Element = (Mass of Element / Mass of Compound) × 100

📖 Meaning: Shows the percentage by mass of an element present in a compound.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate the amount of metal in an ore or compound.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Mass of Element = (% Element × Mass of Compound) ÷ 100

💡 Memory Tip: Percentage compares the part to the whole.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use the mass of the entire compound as the denominator.

Percentage Purity

% Purity = (Mass of Pure Substance / Mass of Sample) × 100

📖 Meaning: Shows how much of a sample is the desired pure substance.

🎯 When to Use: Used in ore analysis and industrial processes.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Mass of Pure Substance = (% Purity × Sample Mass) ÷ 100

💡 Memory Tip: Pure substance is always less than or equal to the sample.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not use impurities as the numerator.

Percentage Recovery

% Recovery = (Mass Recovered / Original Mass) × 100

📖 Meaning: Measures the amount of substance obtained after extraction or purification.

🎯 When to Use: Used in extraction experiments.

📝 Formula:

  • Higher recovery means a more efficient process.

💡 Memory Tip: Recovery compares what you get back with what you started with.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse recovery with purity.

Electrolysis Mass Calculation

m = (MIt) / (zF)

📖 Meaning: Calculates the mass of metal deposited during electrolysis.

🎯 When to Use: Used in extraction of reactive metals.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • m = Mass deposited
  • M = Molar mass
  • I = Current
  • t = Time
  • z = Number of electrons
  • F = Faraday constant

💡 Memory Tip: More current and longer time produce more metal.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use the correct electron number for the metal ion.

Percentage Extraction Efficiency

% Efficiency = (Actual Output / Maximum Possible Output) × 100

📖 Meaning: Measures how effective a metal extraction process is.

🎯 When to Use: Used in industrial extraction calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Actual Output = (% Efficiency × Maximum Output) ÷ 100

💡 Memory Tip: Efficiency is always compared with the ideal result.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Efficiency cannot exceed 100%.

📂 Qualitative Analysis

Solubility Product Constant (Ksp)

Ksp = [Cation]ᵐ × [Anion]ⁿ

📖 Meaning: Solubility product constant shows the equilibrium between a slightly soluble solid and its ions in solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used to determine solubility of ionic compounds.

📝 Formula Example:

  • For AB ⇌ A⁺ + B⁻
  • Ksp = [A⁺][B⁻]

💡 Memory Tip: Include only dissolved ions in Ksp.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not include solid substances.

Ionic Product (Qsp)

Qsp = [Cation]ᵐ × [Anion]ⁿ

📖 Meaning: Ionic product compares current ion concentrations with the solubility equilibrium.

🎯 When to Use: Used to predict precipitation.

📝 Formula Relationship:

  • Qsp < Ksp → Unsaturated solution
  • Qsp = Ksp → Saturated solution
  • Qsp > Ksp → Precipitation occurs

💡 Memory Tip: Compare Qsp with Ksp.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse Qsp with Ksp.

Molar Solubility

S = Moles of Dissolved Solute / Volume of Solution

📖 Meaning: Shows the amount of a substance that dissolves in solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used in solubility calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Moles = Solubility × Volume

💡 Memory Tip: Solubility tells how much can dissolve.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use solution volume, not solvent volume.

Concentration Test Formula

C = n / V

📖 Meaning: Calculates the concentration of ions in a solution.

🎯 When to Use: Used during chemical identification tests.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Moles = Concentration × Volume
  • Volume = Moles ÷ Concentration

💡 Memory Tip: Concentration shows how crowded particles are.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Convert cm³ to dm³ when required.

Flame Test Identification

Metal Ion → Characteristic Flame Colour

📖 Meaning: Different metal ions produce different flame colours when heated.

🎯 When to Use: Used to identify unknown metal ions.

📝 Common Examples:

  • Na⁺ → Yellow flame
  • K⁺ → Lilac flame
  • Ca²⁺ → Brick red flame
  • Cu²⁺ → Blue-green flame

💡 Memory Tip: Each metal has a unique flame signature.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not identify ions using flame colour alone in complex mixtures.

📂 Quantitative Analysis

Moles Formula

n = m / M

📖 Meaning: Calculates the amount of substance in moles from its mass.

🎯 When to Use: Used in chemical calculations involving mass and molar mass.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Moles = Mass ÷ Molar Mass
  • Mass = Moles × Molar Mass
  • Molar Mass = Mass ÷ Moles

💡 Memory Tip: Divide mass by the mass of one mole.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use molar mass in g/mol.

Titration Formula

C₁V₁ / n₁ = C₂V₂ / n₂

📖 Meaning: Relates concentration, volume and mole ratio in a chemical reaction.

🎯 When to Use: Used in acid-base titration calculations.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • C = Concentration
  • V = Volume
  • n = Stoichiometric coefficient

💡 Memory Tip: Balance the equation before calculating.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not ignore the mole ratio.

Molar Volume of Gas

n = V / 24

📖 Meaning: Calculates moles of a gas at room temperature and pressure.

🎯 When to Use: Used for gas volume calculations at RTP.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Volume = Moles × 24 dm³
  • Moles = Volume ÷ 24

💡 Memory Tip: One mole of gas occupies 24 dm³ at RTP.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Convert cm³ to dm³ before calculation.

Percentage Yield

% Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100

📖 Meaning: Measures the efficiency of a chemical reaction.

🎯 When to Use: Used when actual product amount is compared with expected amount.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Actual Yield = (% Yield × Theoretical Yield) ÷ 100

💡 Memory Tip: Real result ÷ expected result.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Yield cannot normally exceed 100%.

Percentage Error

% Error = |Experimental Value − Actual Value| / Actual Value × 100

📖 Meaning: Shows how close an experimental result is to the accepted value.

🎯 When to Use: Used to evaluate accuracy of experiments.

📝 Formula:

  • Smaller error means greater accuracy.

💡 Memory Tip: Error compares difference with the true value.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Always use the actual value as the denominator.

CO₂ Emission = Fuel Used × Emission Factor

Percentage Composition of Air

Greenhouse Effect Calculation

Radiative Effect ∝ Concentration of Greenhouse Gas

📝 Common Greenhouse Gases:

📂 Nuclear Chemistry

Mass-Energy Equivalence

E = mc²

📖 Meaning: Shows the relationship between mass and energy released during nuclear reactions.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate energy produced from mass changes.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • E = Energy
  • m = Mass
  • c = Speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s)

💡 Memory Tip: Small mass changes can produce huge energy.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use mass in kilograms.

Radioactive Decay Law

N = N₀e⁻ˡᵗ

📖 Meaning: Describes how the number of radioactive atoms decreases with time.

🎯 When to Use: Used in radioactive decay calculations.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • N = Amount remaining
  • N₀ = Initial amount
  • λ = Decay constant
  • t = Time

💡 Memory Tip: Radioactive materials decrease over time.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse decay constant with half-life.

Half-Life Formula

N = N₀(1/2)ⁿ

📖 Meaning: Calculates the amount of radioactive material remaining after several half-lives.

🎯 When to Use: Used in radioactive decay problems.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • N = Remaining amount
  • N₀ = Original amount
  • n = Number of half-lives

💡 Memory Tip: Every half-life reduces the amount by half.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Count the number of half-lives correctly.

Half-Life and Decay Constant Relationship

t½ = 0.693 / λ

📖 Meaning: Relates the half-life of a radioactive substance to its decay constant.

🎯 When to Use: Used when converting between half-life and decay constant.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • λ = 0.693 ÷ t½

💡 Memory Tip: Shorter half-life means faster decay.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Keep time units consistent.

Nuclear Binding Energy

Binding Energy = Mass Defect × c²

📖 Meaning: Energy required to separate a nucleus into individual particles.

🎯 When to Use: Used to calculate nuclear stability.

📝 Formula:

  • Mass Defect = Expected Mass − Actual Nuclear Mass

💡 Memory Tip: Greater binding energy means a more stable nucleus.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not ignore the mass defect.

📂 Industrial Chemistry

Percentage Yield

% Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100

📖 Meaning: Measures the efficiency of an industrial chemical process.

🎯 When to Use: Used to compare actual product obtained with the expected amount.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Actual Yield = (% Yield × Theoretical Yield) ÷ 100
  • Theoretical Yield = Actual Yield × 100 ÷ % Yield

💡 Memory Tip: Real product divided by expected product.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Yield cannot normally be above 100%.

Atom Economy

% Atom Economy = (Mr of Desired Product / Total Mr of Reactants) × 100

📖 Meaning: Measures how efficiently atoms from reactants become the desired product.

🎯 When to Use: Used to compare the environmental efficiency of reactions.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Higher atom economy = Less waste

💡 Memory Tip: Good industrial reactions use more atoms.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Use the desired product, not total products.

Reaction Rate

Rate = Amount of Product Formed / Time

📖 Meaning: Shows how quickly an industrial reaction produces a product.

🎯 When to Use: Used when studying efficiency of production.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Amount = Rate × Time
  • Time = Amount ÷ Rate

💡 Memory Tip: Faster rate means more production in less time.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not confuse rate with yield.

Equilibrium Yield Relationship

Higher Pressure → More Product (Gas Reduction Side)

📖 Meaning: Shows the effect of pressure on industrial equilibrium reactions.

🎯 When to Use: Used in processes like ammonia production.

📝 Factors Affecting Yield:

  • Temperature
  • Pressure
  • Catalyst
  • Reactant concentration

💡 Memory Tip: Industrial conditions balance speed and yield.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Catalysts increase speed but do not change equilibrium yield.

Energy Efficiency

% Efficiency = (Useful Energy Output / Total Energy Input) × 100

📖 Meaning: Measures how effectively energy is used in an industrial process.

🎯 When to Use: Used in chemical plants and energy calculations.

📝 Formula Rearrangement:

  • Useful Output = (% Efficiency × Input) ÷ 100

💡 Memory Tip: Efficiency compares useful output with total input.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Energy losses reduce efficiency.

📂 Final Review / Extra Important Chemistry Formulas

Relative Molecular Mass (Mr)

Mr = Sum of Relative Atomic Masses

📖 Meaning: The total mass of all atoms in one molecule.

🎯 When to Use: Used in mole calculations and chemical equations.

📝 Formula Example:

  • H₂O = (2 × 1) + 16 = 18

💡 Memory Tip: Add the atomic masses of all atoms.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Multiply atomic mass by the number of atoms.

Empirical Formula

Empirical Formula = Simplest Whole Number Ratio of Atoms

📖 Meaning: Shows the simplest ratio of elements in a compound.

🎯 When to Use: Used when finding the simplest chemical formula.

📝 Steps:

  • Convert masses to moles.
  • Divide by the smallest mole value.
  • Convert to whole numbers.

💡 Memory Tip: Empirical means simplest.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not leave decimal ratios.

Mole Fraction

X = Moles of Component / Total Moles

📖 Meaning: Represents the fraction of total moles contributed by a substance.

🎯 When to Use: Used in mixtures and gas calculations.

📝 Formula Relationship:

  • Sum of all mole fractions = 1

💡 Memory Tip: Part divided by total.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not use mass instead of moles.

Gas Equation

PV = nRT

📖 Meaning: Relates pressure, volume, temperature and number of moles of a gas.

🎯 When to Use: Used for gases under different conditions.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • P = Pressure
  • V = Volume
  • n = Moles
  • R = Gas constant
  • T = Temperature in Kelvin

💡 Memory Tip: Pressure × Volume equals gas energy terms.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Temperature must be Kelvin.

Avogadro's Constant

Number of Particles = n × Nₐ

📖 Meaning: Converts between moles and number of particles.

🎯 When to Use: Used for atoms, molecules and ions calculations.

📝 Formula Meaning:

  • Nₐ = 6.02 × 10²³ particles/mol

💡 Memory Tip: One mole always contains Avogadro's number of particles.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Do not forget to multiply by the number of moles.
