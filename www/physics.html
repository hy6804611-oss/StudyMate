• A numerical value (number).

• A unit of measurement.

• 5 metres (5 m)

• 20 kilograms (20 kg)

• 30 seconds (30 s)

Physical quantities are divided into two main groups:

1. Fundamental (Base) Quantities

The seven SI base quantities are:

• Length — metre (m)

• Mass — kilogram (kg)

• Time — second (s)

• Electric current — ampere (A)

• Temperature — kelvin (K)

• Amount of substance — mole (mol)

Derived quantities are obtained by combining two or more fundamental quantities.

• Speed — metre per second (m/s)

• Acceleration — metre per second squared (m/s²)

• Pressure — pascal (Pa)

🌍 International System of Units (SI Units)

📘 Chapter 2: Scalars and Vectors

In Physics, many physical quantities are used to describe the motion and behaviour of objects. These quantities are classified into scalars and vectors based on whether they have direction or not. Understanding the difference between scalar and vector quantities is essential because they are used throughout Physics in topics such as motion, force, momentum and electricity.

📖 Meaning of Scalar Quantities

A scalar quantity is a physical quantity that has magnitude (size) only and no direction. A scalar quantity is completely described by its numerical value and unit.

Examples of Scalar Quantities

• Distance

• Speed

• Mass

• Time

• Temperature

• Energy

• Work

• Power

• Volume

• Density

Example:

• A student walks 200 metres.

The quantity 200 metres tells us the distance covered but does not tell us the direction.

🧭 Meaning of Vector Quantities

A vector quantity is a physical quantity that has both magnitude and direction. Both must be stated before the quantity is completely described.

Examples of Vector Quantities

• Displacement

• Velocity

• Acceleration

• Force

• Weight

• Momentum

• Impulse

Example:

• A car moves 80 km/h north.

The magnitude is 80 km/h, while the direction is north.

⚖️ Differences Between Scalars and Vectors

Scalar Quantities

• Have magnitude only.

• Do not have direction.

• Can be added using ordinary arithmetic.


Vector Quantities

• Have both magnitude and direction.

• Direction is important.

• Are added using vector methods.

📘 Chapter 3: Motion

Motion is one of the most important concepts in Physics. Everything around us is either in motion or capable of moving. Cars move on roads, birds fly in the sky, planets revolve around the Sun, and even the Earth rotates on its axis. Understanding motion helps us explain how objects change their positions with time.

📖 Meaning of Motion

Motion is the change in the position of an object with respect to time relative to a reference point or observer.

Examples of Motion

• A car travelling on a highway.

• A football rolling across the field.

• A bird flying in the sky.

• A train moving along a railway track.

• The Earth revolving around the Sun.

🌍 Types of Motion

Translational Motion – Motion in which an object moves from one place to another.

Rotational Motion – Motion in which an object rotates about a fixed axis.

Oscillatory Motion – Motion that repeats itself to and fro about a fixed point.

Random Motion – Motion without a definite direction or pattern.

📏 Distance

Distance is the total length of the path travelled by an object, regardless of direction.

SI Unit: metre (m)

Example: If a student walks 100 m to school and returns 100 m home, the total distance covered is 200 m.

🧭 Displacement

Displacement is the shortest straight-line distance between the initial position and the final position of an object, together with its direction.

SI Unit: metre (m)

Example: If a student walks 100 m to school and returns to the starting point, the displacement is 0 m.

⚡ Speed

Speed is the distance travelled per unit time.

Formula:

Speed = Distance ÷ Time

SI Unit: metre per second (m/s)

Example:

If a car travels 120 metres in 6 seconds:

Speed = 120 ÷ 6 = 20 m/s.

🚗 Velocity

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement with time. It is speed in a specified direction.

Formula:

Velocity = Displacement ÷ Time

SI Unit: metre per second (m/s)

Example:

A car travels east at 25 m/s.

🚀 Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity with time.

Formula:

Acceleration = Change in Velocity ÷ Time

SI Unit: metre per second squared (m/s²)

Example:

A car increases its velocity from 10 m/s to 30 m/s in 5 seconds.

Acceleration = (30 − 10) ÷ 5 = 4 m/s².

📊 Distance-Time Graph

A distance-time graph shows how distance changes with time.

• A straight line indicates constant speed.

• A horizontal line indicates the object is at rest.

• A steeper line indicates greater speed.

📈 Velocity-Time Graph

A velocity-time graph shows how velocity changes with time.

• A horizontal line indicates constant velocity.

• An upward sloping line indicates acceleration.

• A downward sloping line indicates deceleration.

📘 Chapter 4: Force and Newton's Laws of Motion

Force is one of the fundamental concepts in Physics. Every movement, push, pull, collision, or change in the motion of an object is caused by a force. Understanding force helps explain why objects move, stop, accelerate, or change direction. Sir Isaac Newton formulated three important laws that describe how forces affect motion. These laws form the foundation of classical mechanics.

📖 Meaning of Force

Force is a push or pull acting on an object that can change its state of rest, motion, speed, direction, or shape.

SI Unit: Newton (N)

Measuring Instrument: Spring Balance (Newton Meter)

🌍 Effects of Force

A force can:

• Move a stationary object.

• Stop a moving object.

• Increase or decrease the speed of an object.

• Change the direction of motion.

• Change the shape or size of an object.

⚙️ Types of Force

Contact Forces

These forces act only when two objects are in physical contact.

Examples include:

• Frictional Force

• Tension Force

• Normal Reaction Force

• Applied Force


Non-Contact Forces

These forces act without physical contact.

Examples include:

• Gravitational Force

• Magnetic Force

• Electrostatic Force

🌎 Newton's First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia)

A body remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force.

This law explains why passengers move forward when a moving vehicle suddenly stops.

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist any change in its state of rest or motion.

🚀 Newton's Second Law of Motion

The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction of the force.

This law gives the relationship:

Force = Mass × Acceleration

F = ma

Where:

• F = Force (N)

• m = Mass (kg)

• a = Acceleration (m/s²)

Example:

A force acting on a 5 kg object produces an acceleration of 4 m/s².

F = ma

F = 5 × 4

F = 20 N

⚖️ Newton's Third Law of Motion

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

Examples include:

• A gun recoils when fired.

• A swimmer pushes water backward to move forward.

• Rockets move upward by pushing gases downward.

🪨 Mass and Weight

Mass is the quantity of matter contained in an object.

SI Unit: kilogram (kg)

Instrument: Beam Balance


Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object.

Formula:

Weight = Mass × Gravitational Acceleration

W = mg

Where:

• W = Weight (N)

• m = Mass (kg)

• g = 9.8 m/s² (approximately 10 m/s²)

🌍 Applications of Newton's Laws

Newton's laws are applied in:

• Vehicle safety (seat belts and airbags).

• Rocket and spacecraft design.

• Sports and athletics.

• Aviation.

• Mechanical engineering.

• Construction.

⭐ Importance of Force

📘 Chapter 5: Work, Energy and Power

Work, energy and power are closely related concepts in Physics. They explain how forces produce motion, how energy is transferred from one object to another, and how quickly work is done. These concepts are widely applied in engineering, transportation, sports, electricity generation and everyday activities.

📖 Meaning of Work

Work is said to be done when a force applied on an object causes the object to move through a distance in the direction of the force.

Formula:

Work = Force × Distance

W = F × d

SI Unit: Joule (J)

One joule is the work done when a force of 1 newton moves an object through a distance of 1 metre.

🧮 Example of Work

A force of 20 N moves a box through a distance of 5 m.

Work = Force × Distance

Work = 20 × 5

Work = 100 J

⚡ Meaning of Energy

Energy is the ability or capacity to do work.

SI Unit: Joule (J)

Energy cannot be created or destroyed but can only be transformed from one form to another.

🌍 Forms of Energy

Common forms of energy include:

• Mechanical Energy

• Kinetic Energy

• Potential Energy

• Heat (Thermal) Energy

• Electrical Energy

• Chemical Energy

• Light Energy

• Sound Energy

• Nuclear Energy

🏃 Kinetic Energy

Kinetic Energy is the energy possessed by a body because of its motion.

Formula:

KE = ½mv²

Where:

• m = mass (kg)

• v = velocity (m/s)

SI Unit: Joule (J)

⛰️ Potential Energy

Potential Energy is the energy possessed by an object because of its position or height above the ground.

Formula:

PE = mgh

Where:

• m = mass (kg)

• g = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)

• h = height (m)

🔄 Conservation of Energy

The Law of Conservation of Energy states that:

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, but it can be converted from one form to another.

Examples:

• A hydroelectric power station converts potential energy of water into electrical energy.

• A moving car converts chemical energy in fuel into kinetic energy.

• An electric bulb converts electrical energy into light and heat energy.

⚙️ Meaning of Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

Formula:

Power = Work ÷ Time

P = W ÷ t

SI Unit: Watt (W)

One watt is equal to one joule of work done in one second.

🧮 Example of Power

A machine performs 600 J of work in 30 s.

Power = Work ÷ Time

Power = 600 ÷ 30

Power = 20 W

🏭 Applications of Work, Energy and Power

These concepts are applied in:

• Electric power generation.

• Vehicle and engine design.

• Construction and engineering.

• Sports and athletics.

• Manufacturing industries.

• Hydroelectric and wind power stations.

⭐ Importance of Work, Energy and Power

• They explain how machines perform tasks.

• They help engineers design efficient machines.

• They are essential in electricity generation.

• They help calculate machine efficiency.

• They explain energy transformation in nature.

📘 Chapter 6: Momentum and Collisions

Momentum is an important concept in Physics that explains the motion of moving objects and the effects of collisions. It is widely applied in transportation, sports, engineering, aviation, and space technology. Understanding momentum helps explain why heavier or faster-moving objects are more difficult to stop than lighter or slower-moving ones.

📖 Meaning of Momentum

Momentum is the product of the mass of an object and its velocity. It is a vector quantity because it has both magnitude and direction.

Formula:

Momentum = Mass × Velocity

p = mv

Where:

• p = Momentum

• m = Mass (kg)

• v = Velocity (m/s)

SI Unit: kilogram metre per second (kg·m/s)

🧮 Example of Momentum

A body of mass 8 kg moves with a velocity of 6 m/s.

Momentum = Mass × Velocity

Momentum = 8 × 6

Momentum = 48 kg·m/s

⚡ Impulse

Impulse is the product of force and the time during which the force acts. It is equal to the change in momentum.

Formula:

Impulse = Force × Time

I = Ft

Also,

Impulse = Change in Momentum

Ft = mv − mu

SI Unit: Newton-second (N·s) or kg·m/s.

🔄 Principle of Conservation of Momentum

The Principle of Conservation of Momentum states that:

In the absence of external forces, the total momentum of a system remains constant before and after a collision.

This means:

Total Momentum Before Collision = Total Momentum After Collision

🚗 Collisions

A collision occurs when two or more bodies come into contact and exert forces on each other for a short period.

There are two main types of collisions:

1️⃣ Elastic Collision

• Momentum is conserved.

• Kinetic energy is also conserved.

Example: Collision between billiard balls.

2️⃣ Inelastic Collision

• Momentum is conserved.

• Kinetic energy is not conserved.

• Some energy is converted into heat, sound or deformation.

Example: Two vehicles crashing together.

🚙 Applications of Momentum

Momentum is applied in:

• Vehicle safety systems.

• Rocket propulsion.

• Sports such as football, boxing and cricket.

• Ballistics.

• Aircraft design.

• Industrial machinery.

🛡️ Safety Devices Based on Momentum

Some devices reduce the effects of collisions by increasing the time taken to stop a moving object.

Examples include:

• Seat belts.

• Airbags.

• Crash helmets.

• Vehicle bumpers.

• Safety nets.

• Packing materials.

⭐ Importance of Momentum

• Helps explain collisions.

• Improves transportation safety.

• Assists engineers in machine design.

• Supports the design of rockets and spacecraft.

• Helps scientists understand moving bodies.

📘 Chapter 7: Circular Motion and Gravitation

Many objects in nature move in circular paths. The Earth revolves around the Sun, the Moon revolves around the Earth, satellites orbit the Earth, and vehicles move around bends on roads. The study of circular motion and gravitation helps explain these motions and the forces responsible for them.

🔄 Circular Motion

Circular motion is the motion of an object along a circular path about a fixed centre.

Examples of Circular Motion

• The blades of a ceiling fan.

• A stone tied to a string and whirled in a circle.

• The hands of a clock.

• The wheels of a moving bicycle.

• Artificial satellites orbiting the Earth.

🎯 Centripetal Force

Centripetal force is the force that keeps an object moving in a circular path. It always acts towards the centre of the circle.

Formula:

F = mv²/r

Where:

• F = Centripetal force (N)

• m = Mass (kg)

• v = Velocity (m/s)

• r = Radius of the circular path (m)

SI Unit: Newton (N)

🚗 Applications of Centripetal Force

Examples include:

• Vehicles moving safely around bends.

• Satellites orbiting the Earth.

• Roller coaster rides.

• Washing machine spin dryers.

• Merry-go-rounds.

🌍 Gravitation

Gravitation is the force of attraction between any two masses in the universe.

Sir Isaac Newton explained gravitation through the Universal Law of Gravitation.

📖 Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation

The law states that:

Every two bodies in the universe attract each other with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.

Formula:

F = Gm₁m₂/r²

Where:

• F = Gravitational force.

• G = Universal gravitational constant.

• m₁ and m₂ = Masses of the two bodies.

• r = Distance between their centres.

⚖️ Gravitational Field

A gravitational field is the region around a body where another body experiences gravitational attraction.

The Earth has a gravitational field that pulls objects towards its centre.

⬇️ Acceleration Due to Gravity

The acceleration produced by the Earth's gravitational pull is called acceleration due to gravity (g).

Value:

g = 9.8 m/s² (approximately 10 m/s²).

🌕 Artificial Satellites

An artificial satellite is a human-made object placed in orbit around the Earth or another celestial body.

Examples include:

• Communication satellites.

• Weather satellites.

• Navigation satellites (GPS).

• Scientific research satellites.

🚀 Applications of Gravitation

Gravitation is important in:

📘 Chapter 8: Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) is a special type of oscillatory motion that occurs in many natural and man-made systems. The swinging of a pendulum, the vibration of a guitar string, the movement of a spring, and the oscillation of a tuning fork are all examples of Simple Harmonic Motion. SHM is widely applied in clocks, musical instruments, engineering, and scientific instruments.

📖 Meaning of Oscillatory Motion

Oscillatory motion is the repeated movement of an object to and fro about a fixed or equilibrium position.

Examples include:

• A swinging pendulum.

• A vibrating guitar string.

• A child on a swing.

• A vibrating tuning fork.

• A mass attached to a spring.

⏱️ Time Period

The time period (T) is the time taken for one complete oscillation.

SI Unit: second (s)

🔁 Frequency

Frequency (f) is the number of complete oscillations made in one second.

Formula:

f = 1/T

Where:

• f = Frequency (Hz)

• T = Time Period (s)

SI Unit: Hertz (Hz)

📐 Angular Velocity (Angular Frequency)

Angular velocity (or angular frequency) is the rate at which the oscillating body moves through an angle.

Formula:

ω = 2πf

SI Unit: rad/s

🪂 Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum consists of a small heavy bob suspended by a light, inextensible string from a fixed support so that it can swing freely.

The motion of a simple pendulum approximates Simple Harmonic Motion when the angle of swing is small.

🌀 Mass-Spring System

A body attached to a spring performs Simple Harmonic Motion when displaced from its equilibrium position and released.

The spring exerts a restoring force that always acts towards the equilibrium position.

⚙️ Characteristics of SHM

• The motion is periodic.

• The body moves to and fro repeatedly.

• The restoring force always acts towards the equilibrium position.

• The acceleration is greatest at the extreme positions.

• The velocity is greatest at the equilibrium position.

🌍 Applications of SHM

Simple Harmonic Motion is applied in:

• Pendulum clocks.

• Mechanical watches.

• Musical instruments.

• Seismographs.

• Vehicle suspension systems.

• Engineering vibration analysis.

• Scientific measuring instruments.

⭐ Importance of SHM

• Explains vibrations in nature.

• Helps engineers design stable structures.

• Used in timekeeping devices.

• Essential in the study of waves and sound.

• Helps scientists understand periodic motion.

📘 Chapter 9: Equilibrium and Centre of Gravity

Equilibrium is an important concept in Physics and Engineering. Many structures such as bridges, buildings, cranes, ladders and towers are designed based on the principles of equilibrium. Understanding equilibrium and the centre of gravity helps us explain why some objects remain stable while others topple over.

📖 Meaning of Equilibrium

Equilibrium is the state in which all the forces acting on a body are balanced, resulting in no change in its state of rest or uniform motion.

An object is said to be in equilibrium when:

• The resultant force acting on it is zero.

• The resultant turning effect (moment) is zero.

⚖️ Types of Equilibrium

Stable Equilibrium

An object is in stable equilibrium if, after being slightly displaced, it returns to its original position.

Examples:

• A cone resting on its base.

• A marble at the bottom of a bowl.


Unstable Equilibrium

An object is in unstable equilibrium if, after being slightly displaced, it moves farther away from its original position.

Examples:

• A cone balanced on its tip.

• A marble placed on top of a hill.


Neutral Equilibrium

An object is in neutral equilibrium if, after being displaced, it remains in its new position.

Examples:

• A ball resting on a flat horizontal surface.

• A cylinder lying on its curved surface.

📍 Centre of Gravity

The centre of gravity (C.G.) is the point through which the entire weight of a body appears to act.

For a uniform body, the centre of gravity is usually at its geometric centre.

📏 Factors Affecting Stability

The stability of an object depends mainly on:

• The position of its centre of gravity.

• The size of its base.

An object is more stable when:

• It has a low centre of gravity.

• It has a wide base.

🔄 Moment of a Force (Torque)

The moment of a force is the turning effect produced by a force acting about a pivot or fixed point.

Formula:

Moment = Force × Perpendicular Distance

M = F × d

SI Unit: Newton metre (N·m)

🧮 Worked Example 1

A force of 30 N acts perpendicular to a spanner at a distance of 0.4 m from the pivot. Calculate the moment of the force.

Solution

Moment = Force × Distance

M = 30 × 0.4

M = 12 N·m

⚖️ Principle of Moments

The Principle of Moments states that:

For a body in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about a pivot is equal to the sum of the anticlockwise moments about the same pivot.

🧮 Worked Example 2

A boy applies a force of 50 N at one end of a seesaw, 2 m from the pivot. What is the clockwise moment?

Solution

M = F × d

M = 50 × 2

M = 100 N·m

📘 Chapter 10: Elasticity

Elasticity is the property of a material that enables it to return to its original shape and size after the force causing deformation has been removed. Materials behave differently when forces act on them. Some return to their original shape, while others remain permanently deformed. The study of elasticity is important in engineering, construction, vehicle design, and manufacturing.

📖 Elasticity

Elasticity is the ability of a material to regain its original shape and size after the applied force is removed.

Examples:

• Rubber band.

• Steel spring.

• Elastic rope.

📖 Plasticity

Plasticity is the property of a material that causes it to remain permanently deformed after the applied force is removed.

Examples:

• Putty.

• Wet cement.

• Soft wax.

📏 Stress

Stress is the force acting per unit cross-sectional area of a material.

Formula:

Stress = Force ÷ Area

σ = F/A

SI Unit: Pascal (Pa) or N/m².

📐 Strain

Strain is the ratio of the change in length of a material to its original length.

Formula:

Strain = Change in Length ÷ Original Length

Strain = ΔL/L

Strain has no SI unit because it is a ratio.

⚖️ Hooke's Law

Hooke's Law states that:

Provided the elastic limit is not exceeded, the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the applied force.

0

Formula:

F = kx

Where:

• F = Force (N)

• k = Spring constant (N/m)

• x = Extension (m)

📍 Elastic Limit

The elastic limit is the maximum force a material can withstand and still return to its original shape when the force is removed.

If this limit is exceeded, permanent deformation occurs.

📘 Chapter 11: Pressure

Pressure is an important concept in Physics that explains how force acts on surfaces. It is applied in hydraulic machines, vehicle braking systems, construction, medicine, aviation, weather forecasting and many everyday activities. Understanding pressure helps explain why sharp objects cut easily, why dams are thicker at the bottom, and how hydraulic systems multiply force.

📖 Meaning of Pressure

Pressure is the force acting normally (perpendicularly) per unit area of a surface.

Formula:

Pressure = Force ÷ Area

P = F/A

Where:

• P = Pressure (Pa)

• F = Force (N)

• A = Area (m²)

SI Unit: Pascal (Pa)

One Pascal is the pressure produced when a force of one Newton acts on an area of one square metre.

🧮 Worked Example 1

A force of 200 N acts on an area of 4 m². Calculate the pressure.

Solution

P = F/A

P = 200 ÷ 4

P = 50 Pa

📌 Factors Affecting Pressure

Pressure depends on:

• The magnitude of the applied force.

• The area over which the force acts.

Increasing the force increases pressure.

Increasing the area decreases pressure.

🔪 Applications of Pressure in Solids

Examples include:

• Sharp knives cut easily because they have small surface areas.

• Needles and pins easily penetrate materials.

• High-heeled shoes exert greater pressure than flat shoes.

• Tractors use wide tyres to reduce pressure on soft ground.

💧 Pressure in Liquids

Liquids exert pressure in all directions. The pressure in a liquid increases with depth.

Formula:

P = ρgh

Where:

• ρ = Density of the liquid (kg/m³)

• g = Acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)

• h = Depth of the liquid (m)

SI Unit: Pascal (Pa)

🧮 Worked Example 2

Solution

P = ρgh

P = 1000 × 10 × 5

P = 50,000 Pa

🌊 Atmospheric Pressure

Atmospheric pressure is the pressure exerted on the Earth's surface by the weight of the atmosphere.

Standard Atmospheric Pressure:

101,325 Pa (approximately 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa).

Instrument Used: Barometer.

⚙️ Pascal's Principle

Pascal's Principle states that:

Pressure applied to an enclosed liquid is transmitted equally and undiminished in all directions throughout the liquid.

0

📘 Chapter 12: Heat and Temperature

Heat and temperature are fundamental concepts in Physics. Although they are closely related, they have different meanings. These concepts are essential in engineering, medicine, meteorology, refrigeration, cooking, and many industrial processes.

📖 Meaning of Heat

Heat is a form of energy that is transferred from a body at a higher temperature to another body at a lower temperature until thermal equilibrium is reached.

SI Unit: Joule (J)

Heat always flows naturally from a hotter object to a colder object.

🌡️ Meaning of Temperature

Temperature is the degree of hotness or coldness of a body. It is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles of a substance.

SI Unit: Kelvin (K)

Temperature is also commonly measured in degrees Celsius (°C).

⚖️ Difference Between Heat and Temperature

Heat

• It is a form of energy.

• It flows from hot objects to cold objects.

• It is measured in Joules (J).


Temperature

• It measures the degree of hotness or coldness.

• It does not flow.

• It is measured in Kelvin (K) or degrees Celsius (°C).

🌡️ Thermometers

📘 Chapter 13: Thermal Expansion

Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled. This phenomenon is known as thermal expansion. Understanding thermal expansion is important in engineering, construction, transportation, and manufacturing because it helps prevent damage to structures and machines caused by changes in temperature.

📖 Meaning of Thermal Expansion

Thermal expansion is the increase in the dimensions (length, area, or volume) of a substance when its temperature increases.

When a substance is cooled, it undergoes thermal contraction, which is the decrease in its dimensions.

🌡️ Why Do Materials Expand?

When heat is supplied to a material, its particles gain kinetic energy and vibrate more rapidly. As a result, the particles move farther apart, causing the material to expand.

📏 Types of Thermal Expansion

1. Linear Expansion

Linear expansion is the increase in the length of a solid when heated.

Examples:

• Railway tracks.

• Metal rods.

• Electric cables.


2. Superficial (Area) Expansion

Superficial expansion is the increase in the surface area of a material when heated.

Examples:

• Metal plates.

• Thin sheets of aluminium.


3. Cubical (Volume) Expansion

Cubical expansion is the increase in the volume of a substance when heated.

Examples:

• Liquids in containers.

• Gases in cylinders.

• Solid metal blocks.

🧮 Linear Expansion Formula

Formula:

ΔL = αL₀ΔT

Where:

• ΔL = Change in length (m)

• α = Linear expansivity (/°C)

• L₀ = Original length (m)

• ΔT = Change in temperature (°C)

🧮 Worked Example 1

A steel rod has an original length of 2 m. It expands by 0.004 m after heating. Calculate its new length.

Solution

New Length = Original Length + Increase in Length

New Length = 2 + 0.004

New Length = 2.004 m

🌍 Effects of Thermal Expansion

Useful Effects

• Manufacture of thermometers.

• Hot riveting in engineering.

• Bimetallic strips used in thermostats.

• Shrink fitting of machine parts.


Harmful Effects

• Railway tracks may buckle during hot weather.

• Bridges may crack if expansion joints are not provided.

• Electric wires may sag during hot seasons.

• Glass containers may crack when heated suddenly.

🛠️ Prevention of Thermal Expansion Problems

Engineers reduce the harmful effects by:

• Providing expansion joints on bridges.

• Leaving gaps between railway tracks.

• Hanging electric cables loosely.

• Using rollers beneath long bridges.

🧮 Worked Example 2

A metal rod measures 5 m at room temperature. If it expands by 0.01 m after heating, calculate its final length.

Solution

Final Length = Original Length + Expansion

Final Length = 5 + 0.01

Final Length = 5.01 m

📘 Chapter 14: Transfer of Heat

Heat is continuously transferred from one place to another in nature and in everyday life. We feel the warmth of the Sun, boil water for cooking, use refrigerators to preserve food, and rely on heating and cooling systems in homes and industries. Understanding how heat is transferred is essential in engineering, medicine, meteorology, construction and many other fields.

📖 Meaning of Heat Transfer

Heat transfer is the movement of heat energy from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature until both regions reach the same temperature.

There are three main methods of heat transfer:

• Conduction.

• Convection.

• Radiation.

🔥 Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of heat through a material without the actual movement of the material itself.

Conduction occurs mainly in solids because their particles are closely packed together.

Examples:

• A metal spoon becoming hot in a cup of tea.

• Ironing clothes with a hot electric iron.

• Heating one end of a metal rod causes the other end to become hot.

🧱 Conductors and Insulators

Good Conductors of Heat

• Copper.

• Aluminium.

• Silver.

• Iron.


Poor Conductors (Insulators)

• Wood.

• Plastic.

• Rubber.

• Glass.

• Air.

🌊 Convection

Convection is the transfer of heat through the movement of fluids (liquids and gases).

When a fluid is heated, it expands, becomes less dense and rises, while cooler, denser fluid sinks. This continuous movement forms convection currents.

Examples:

• Boiling water in a pot.

• Sea breeze and land breeze.

• Ventilation in buildings.

• Hot air balloons.

☀️ Radiation

Radiation is the transfer of heat in the form of electromagnetic waves without the need for a material medium.

Radiation can occur through a vacuum.

Examples:

• Heat from the Sun reaching the Earth.

• Feeling warmth from a fire.

• Electric heaters.

🌑 Good Absorbers and Good Reflectors

Good Absorbers of Heat

• Dull black surfaces absorb heat quickly.


Good Reflectors of Heat

• Shiny silver or polished surfaces reflect heat effectively.

This is why vacuum flasks have shiny inner surfaces to reduce heat loss by radiation.

🧮 Worked Example 1

Solution

Since heat moves through the solid spoon without the spoon itself moving, the mode of heat transfer is:

Conduction.

🧮 Worked Example 2

Solution

The hot air and smoke become less dense when heated and rise upward, while cooler air moves in to replace them. This movement is known as a convection current.

📘 Chapter 15: Change of State and Latent Heat

Matter exists mainly in three physical states: solid, liquid and gas. Under suitable conditions of heating or cooling, substances can change from one state to another. 📖 States of Matter

The three common states of matter are:

• Solid.

• Liquid.

• Gas.

Each state differs in the arrangement, movement and spacing of its particles.

🔄 Change of State

A change of state is the conversion of a substance from one physical state to another due to the gain or loss of heat energy.

❄️ Melting

Melting is the process by which a solid changes into a liquid when heated to its melting point.

Example:

• Ice melting into water.

🧊 Freezing (Solidification)

Freezing is the process by which a liquid changes into a solid when cooled to its freezing point.

Example:

• Water freezing to form ice.

💨 Evaporation

Evaporation is the gradual change of a liquid into vapour from its surface at any temperature below its boiling point.

Factors affecting evaporation include:

• Temperature.

• Surface area.

• Wind speed.

• Humidity.

♨️ Boiling

Boiling is the rapid conversion of a liquid into vapour throughout the liquid at a fixed temperature called the boiling point.

Example:

• Water boiling at 100°C under normal atmospheric pressure.

☁️ Condensation

Condensation is the change of a gas or vapour into a liquid when cooled.

Examples:

• Formation of dew.

• Water droplets on a cold bottle.

🌫️ Sublimation

Sublimation is the direct change of a solid into a gas without passing through the liquid state.

Examples:

• Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide).

• Iodine crystals.

• Naphthalene balls.

🔥 Latent Heat

Latent heat is the heat absorbed or released during a change of state without any change in temperature.

• Lv = Specific latent heat of vaporization (J/kg)

📘 Chapter :21 Electromagnetism

Electromagnetism is one of the most important topics in Physics because it explains how electricity and magnetism work together. 📖 Meaning of Electromagnetism

Electromagnetism is the interaction between electricity and magnetism. It explains how electric current produces a magnetic field and how a changing magnetic field can produce electricity.

🧲 Magnetic Field Around a Current-Carrying Conductor

Whenever electric current flows through a conductor, a magnetic field is produced around it.

The direction of the magnetic field is determined using the Right-Hand Grip Rule.

Right-Hand Grip Rule

• Hold the conductor with your right hand.

• The thumb points in the direction of current.

• The curled fingers show the direction of the magnetic field.

🌀 Solenoid

A solenoid is a long cylindrical coil of insulated wire wound in the form of a helix.

When current flows through a solenoid, it behaves like a bar magnet.

⚡ Electromagnetic Induction

Electromagnetic induction is the production of an electromotive force (e.m.f.) in a conductor whenever it cuts magnetic field lines or when the magnetic flux linked with it changes.

This principle was discovered by Michael Faraday.

📜 Faraday's Laws of Electromagnetic Induction

First Law

An e.m.f. is induced whenever there is a change in magnetic flux linking a conductor.

Second Law

The magnitude of the induced e.m.f. is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux.

Formula:

E = ΔΦ / Δt

Where:

• E = Induced e.m.f. (Volt)

• ΔΦ = Change in magnetic flux (Weber)

• Δt = Time (seconds)

🧭 Fleming's Left-Hand Rule

This rule is used to determine the direction of motion in an electric motor.

• First Finger → Magnetic Field.

• Second Finger → Current.

• Thumb → Motion (Force).

🧭 Fleming's Right-Hand Rule

This rule is used to determine the direction of induced current in a generator.

• First Finger → Magnetic Field.

• Thumb → Motion.

• Second Finger → Induced Current.

Vp / Vs = Np / Ns

• Vp = Primary voltage

• Vs = Secondary voltage

• Np = Number of turns in the primary coil

• Ns = Number of turns in the secondary coil

• Can be stopped by paper.

• Highest ionising power.


2. Beta (β) Particles

• Negatively charged electrons.

• Moderate penetrating power.

• Can be stopped by thin aluminium sheets.

• Moderate ionising power.


3. Gamma (γ) Rays

• No charge.

• Travel at the speed of light.

• Highest penetrating power.

⚛️ Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay is a random process during which unstable nuclei lose energy by emitting radiation until they become stable.

⏳ Half-Life

Half-life is the time taken for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay.

Symbol: T½

After one half-life → 1/2 remains.

After two half-lives → 1/4 remains.

After three half-lives → 1/8 remains.

☢️ Nuclear Fission

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two lighter nuclei with the release of a large amount of energy.

Applications

• Nuclear power stations.

• Electricity generation.

☀️ Nuclear Fusion

Nuclear fusion is the combination of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus with the release of enormous energy.

Fusion occurs naturally in the Sun and stars.

⚠️ Hazards of Radioactivity

• Cancer.

• Genetic mutations.

• Radiation burns.

• Death from excessive exposure.

🛡️ Safety Precautions

• Wear protective clothing.

• Use lead shielding.

• Minimise exposure time.

• Maximise distance from radioactive sources.

• Store radioactive materials properly.

📘 Chapter 26: Semiconductor Devices

Semiconductor devices are the foundation of modern electronic technology. They are found in computers, mobile phones, calculators, televisions, medical equipment, satellites and almost every electronic system used today. Understanding semiconductors helps students appreciate how modern electronic devices function.

📖 Meaning of a Semiconductor

🖼️ Diagram: Conductor, Semiconductor and Insulator

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator.

Its conductivity can be increased by adding suitable impurities, a process known as doping.

🧪 Examples of Semiconductor Materials

• Silicon (Si).

• Germanium (Ge).

Silicon is the most widely used semiconductor because it is abundant, stable and inexpensive.

⚙️ Types of Semiconductors

1. Intrinsic Semiconductor

An intrinsic semiconductor is a pure semiconductor without any added impurities.


2. Extrinsic Semiconductor

An extrinsic semiconductor is formed by adding impurities to increase conductivity.

🔵 N-Type Semiconductor

An N-type semiconductor is produced by doping silicon or germanium with pentavalent elements such as:

• Phosphorus.

• Arsenic.

• Antimony.

In N-type semiconductors, electrons are the majority charge carriers.

🔴 P-Type Semiconductor

A P-type semiconductor is produced by doping silicon or germanium with trivalent elements such as:

• Boron.

• Aluminium.

• Gallium.

In P-type semiconductors, holes are the majority charge carriers.

➡️ P-N Junction

🖼️ Diagram: P-N Junction

A P-N Junction is formed when P-type and N-type semiconductors are joined together.

This junction forms the basis of:

• Diodes.

• LEDs.

• Solar cells.

• Transistors.

⚡ Forward Bias

In forward bias:

• Positive terminal is connected to the P-side.

• Negative terminal is connected to the N-side.

Current flows easily through the diode.

⛔ Reverse Bias

In reverse bias:

• Positive terminal is connected to the N-side.

• Negative terminal is connected to the P-side.

Very little or no current flows.

☀️ Solar Cell

🖼️ Diagram: Solar Cell

A solar cell converts sunlight directly into electrical energy using the photovoltaic effect.

Applications

• Solar panels.

• Calculators.

• Satellites.

• Street lights.
