🧪 CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION TO CHEMISTRY

Introduction

Chemistry is one of the major branches of science that deals with the study of matter, its composition, structure, properties and the changes it undergoes. Everything around us, including air, water, food, medicines, metals and living organisms, is made up of matter. Chemistry helps us understand how substances interact and how they can be used to improve our daily lives.

The knowledge of Chemistry has contributed greatly to medicine, agriculture, engineering, environmental protection, food production and many other fields. It is therefore an important subject for students preparing for WAEC, NECO, JAMB, IGCSE and other international examinations.

Meaning of Chemistry

Chemistry is the branch of science that studies the composition, structure, properties and changes of matter.

Meaning of Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Examples: Water, air, wood, iron, plastic, stone, paper and oxygen.

Importance of Chemistry

• Production of medicines.

• Manufacture of fertilizers.

• Food preservation and processing.

• Production of plastics, soaps and detergents.

• Water purification.

• Environmental protection.

• Manufacture of fuels.

• Production of building materials.

• Development of modern technology.

Branches of Chemistry

Organic Chemistry
Studies carbon compounds and hydrocarbons.

Inorganic Chemistry
Studies substances that are not mainly carbon compounds.

Physical Chemistry
Studies the physical properties and behaviour of chemical substances.

Analytical Chemistry
Deals with the identification and analysis of chemical substances.

Biochemistry

Applications of Chemistry in Everyday Life

Chemistry plays an important role in our daily lives. Many products and processes we use every day are based on chemical principles.

• Food preservation and processing.

🧪 CHAPTER 2: PARTICULATE NATURE OF MATTER

Introduction

Everything around us is made up of tiny particles. These particles are too small to be seen with the naked eye but are constantly moving. The particulate nature of matter explains the properties of solids, liquids and gases, as well as many physical and chemical processes such as diffusion, evaporation and dissolution.

Particle Theory of Matter

The Particle Theory of Matter states that all matter is made up of tiny particles called atoms, molecules or ions. These particles are in constant random motion and have spaces between them.

Main Postulates of the Particle Theory

• All matter is made up of tiny particles.

• The particles are in constant random motion.

• There are spaces between the particles.

• The particles attract one another.

• Heating increases the movement of particles, while cooling reduces their movement.

States of Matter

Matter exists in three main physical states: solid, liquid and gas. The arrangement and movement of particles determine the properties of each state.

Solid

In solids, particles are closely packed together and held by strong forces of attraction. They vibrate about fixed positions but cannot move freely.

Properties of Solids

• Have a definite shape.

• Have a definite volume.

• Cannot be compressed easily.

• Particles are closely packed.

• High density.

Examples: Iron, wood, stone, ice and salt.

Liquid

In liquids, particles are close together but can move past one another. This allows liquids to flow and take the shape of their container.

Properties of Liquids

• Have no fixed shape.

• Have a definite volume.

• Flow easily.

• Cannot be compressed easily.

• Particles move freely.

Examples: Water, milk, oil and ethanol.

Gas

In gases, particles are far apart and move rapidly in all directions. The attractive forces between particles are very weak.

Properties of Gases

• Have no fixed shape.

• Have no fixed volume.

• Can be compressed easily.

• Expand to fill any container.

• Particles move randomly at high speed.

Examples: Oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide and hydrogen.

Comparison of the Three States of Matter

Property Solid Liquid Gas
Shape Definite Takes the shape of its container No definite shape
Volume Definite Definite No definite volume
Particle Arrangement Closely packed Closely packed but movable Far apart
Particle Movement Vibrate in fixed positions Move past one another Move freely in all directions
Compressibility Very low Very low High
Density High Moderate Low

Changes of State

A change of state occurs when matter changes from one physical state to another due to heating or cooling. During these changes, no new substance is formed.

Melting
The change of a solid into a liquid when heated.

Example: Ice changing into water.

Freezing (Solidification)
The change of a liquid into a solid when cooled.

Example: Water changing into ice.

Evaporation
The slow change of a liquid into a gas from its surface at any temperature.

Example: Drying of wet clothes.

Boiling
The rapid change of a liquid into a gas throughout the liquid at its boiling point.

Example: Boiling water to produce steam.

Condensation
The change of a gas into a liquid when cooled.

Example: Formation of water droplets on a cold bottle.

Sublimation
The direct change of a solid into a gas without passing through the liquid state.

Examples: Iodine crystals, dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) and naphthalene.

Brownian Motion

Brownian motion is the continuous random movement of tiny particles suspended in a liquid or gas due to constant collisions with the fast-moving particles of the surrounding medium.

Examples

• Movement of pollen grains in water.

• Movement of smoke particles in air.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration until they are evenly distributed.

Examples

• The smell of perfume spreading throughout a room.

• The smell of cooking reaching different parts of a house.

• A drop of ink spreading in water.

Factors Affecting Diffusion

• Temperature.

• Concentration difference.

• Size of particles.

• Nature of the medium (gas, liquid or solid).

Kinetic Theory of Matter

The Kinetic Theory of Matter explains the behaviour of matter in terms of the movement of its particles.

Main Points

• Matter consists of tiny particles.

• The particles are always moving.

• Higher temperature increases the kinetic energy of particles.

• Particles collide continuously with one another.

• The attractive forces between particles differ in solids, liquids and gases.

Evidence for the Particulate Nature of Matter

• Diffusion of gases.

• Diffusion of liquids.

• Brownian motion.

• Expansion of substances when heated.

• Compression of gases.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking the particles of matter are motionless.

✅ Particles of matter are in constant random motion, even in solids.

❌ Believing gases have no mass because they cannot be seen.

✅ Gases have mass and occupy space, so they are forms of matter.

❌ Confusing evaporation with boiling.

✅ Evaporation occurs only at the surface of a liquid and can happen at any temperature, while boiling occurs throughout the liquid at its boiling point.

❌ Thinking diffusion occurs only in gases.

✅ Diffusion occurs in gases, liquids and even solids, although it is fastest in gases.

❌ Believing melting and dissolving are the same process.

✅ Melting is the change of a solid into a liquid by heating, while dissolving is the mixing of a substance with a solvent to form a solution.

❌ Thinking heating creates particles.

✅ Heating only increases the kinetic energy and movement of existing particles.

⭐ State the postulates of the particle theory of matter.

⭐ Compare the properties of solids, liquids and gases.

⭐ Explain the different changes of state with suitable examples.

⭐ Explain the effect of temperature on particle movement.

⭐ Examiners frequently ask students to compare the three states of matter, explain diffusion, identify changes of state and apply the kinetic theory to everyday phenomena.

⚛️ CHAPTER 3: ATOMIC STRUCTURE

Introduction

All matter is made up of tiny particles called atoms. The atom is the basic unit of an element and is responsible for its chemical properties. Understanding atomic structure helps explain how elements combine to form compounds and participate in chemical reactions.

Meaning of an Atom

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction while retaining the chemical properties of that element.

Structure of an Atom

An atom consists of a central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, while electrons move around the nucleus in energy levels (shells).

Subatomic Particles

Atoms are made up of three main subatomic particles: protons, neutrons and electrons.

1. Proton

A proton is a positively charged particle found in the nucleus of an atom.

Properties of Protons

• Positive charge (+1).

• Located in the nucleus.

• Relative mass of 1 atomic mass unit (amu).

• Determines the atomic number of an element.

2. Neutron

A neutron is a neutral particle found in the nucleus of an atom.

Properties of Neutrons

• No electrical charge.

• Located in the nucleus.

• Relative mass of 1 atomic mass unit (amu).

• Contributes to the mass of an atom.

3. Electron

An electron is a negatively charged particle that moves around the nucleus in energy levels or shells.

Properties of Electrons

• Negative charge (–1).

• Found outside the nucleus.

• Very small mass (about 1/1836 amu).

• Responsible for chemical bonding and chemical reactions.

Comparison of Subatomic Particles

Particle Symbol Charge Location Relative Mass
Proton p⁺ +1 Nucleus 1
Neutron n⁰ 0 Nucleus 1
Electron e⁻ –1 Outside the nucleus 1/1836

Atomic Number

The atomic number of an element is the number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom. It is represented by the symbol Z.

In a neutral atom, the number of protons is equal to the number of electrons.

Example:

• Hydrogen has 1 proton, so its atomic number is 1.

• Carbon has 6 protons, so its atomic number is 6.

• Oxygen has 8 protons, so its atomic number is 8.

Mass Number

The mass number of an atom is the total number of protons and neutrons in its nucleus. It is represented by the symbol A.

Mass Number = Number of Protons + Number of Neutrons

Calculating the Number of Neutrons

The number of neutrons can be calculated using the formula:

Number of Neutrons = Mass Number − Atomic Number

Example 1

Sodium (Na)

Atomic Number = 11

Mass Number = 23

Number of Neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12

Example 2

Oxygen (O)

Atomic Number = 8

Mass Number = 16

Number of Neutrons = 16 − 8 = 8

Nuclide Notation

Atoms are often represented using nuclide notation, where the mass number is written above the atomic number.

Examples:

¹₂H    Hydrogen

¹²₆C    Carbon-12

¹⁶₈O    Oxygen-16

²³₁₁Na    Sodium-23

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same atomic number but different mass numbers because they contain different numbers of neutrons.

Examples of Isotopes

• Hydrogen-1 (Protium).

• Hydrogen-2 (Deuterium).

• Hydrogen-3 (Tritium).

• Carbon-12 and Carbon-14.

• Chlorine-35 and Chlorine-37.

Uses of Isotopes

Isotopes have many important applications in medicine, agriculture, industry and scientific research.

Applications of Isotopes

• Carbon-14 is used for dating fossils and archaeological remains.

• Iodine-131 is used to diagnose and treat thyroid diseases.

• Cobalt-60 is used in the treatment of cancer (radiotherapy).

• Uranium-235 is used as fuel in nuclear power stations.

• Technetium-99m is used in medical imaging.

Ions

An ion is an atom or group of atoms that carries an electrical charge because it has gained or lost one or more electrons.

Types of Ions

1. Cation
A cation is a positively charged ion formed when an atom loses one or more electrons.

Examples: Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺.

2. Anion
An anion is a negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains one or more electrons.

Examples: Cl⁻, O²⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻.

Formation of Ions

Formation of a Sodium Ion

A sodium atom loses one electron to form a sodium ion.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Formation of a Chloride Ion

A chlorine atom gains one electron to form a chloride ion.

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Atomic Models

Scientists have proposed different models to explain the structure of the atom.

John Dalton's Atomic Model
Matter is made up of tiny, indivisible atoms.

J. J. Thomson's Atomic Model
The atom is a positively charged sphere with electrons embedded in it ("Plum Pudding Model").

Ernest Rutherford's Atomic Model
The atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus surrounded by electrons.

Niels Bohr's Atomic Model
Electrons move around the nucleus in fixed energy levels or shells.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking the atomic number is the same as the mass number.

✅ The atomic number is the number of protons, while the mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons.

❌ Believing electrons are found inside the nucleus.

✅ Electrons occupy energy levels (shells) surrounding the nucleus.

❌ Thinking isotopes are different elements.

✅ Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different numbers of neutrons.

❌ Confusing cations with anions.

✅ Cations are positively charged because they lose electrons, while anions are negatively charged because they gain electrons.

❌ Believing atoms always carry electrical charges.

✅ Ordinary atoms are electrically neutral because the number of protons equals the number of electrons.

❌ Thinking neutrons have a positive charge.

✅ Neutrons have no electrical charge.

Exam Tips

⭐ Define atom, atomic number, mass number, isotope and ion correctly.

⭐ Know the properties of protons, neutrons and electrons.

⭐ Memorize the formulas:

Mass Number = Protons + Neutrons

Neutrons = Mass Number − Atomic Number

⭐ Differentiate between cations and anions.

⭐ Know the uses of important isotopes.

⭐ Understand the contributions of Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr to atomic theory.

⭐ Examiners frequently ask students to calculate atomic number, mass number and neutrons, identify ions and explain isotopes.

⚛️ CHAPTER 4: ELECTRONIC CONFIGURATION

Introduction

Electronic configuration is the arrangement of electrons in the energy levels (shells) around the nucleus of an atom. The arrangement of electrons determines the chemical properties, valency and reactivity of an element.

Energy Levels (Electron Shells)

Electrons occupy specific energy levels, also called electron shells, around the nucleus. These shells are represented by the letters K, L, M, N, O, P and Q.

The maximum number of electrons each shell can hold is:

• K Shell = 2 electrons.

• L Shell = 8 electrons.

• M Shell = 18 electrons.

• N Shell = 32 electrons.

For the first 20 elements, electrons are commonly arranged using the simple pattern 2, 8, 8, 2.

Rules for Writing Electronic Configuration

Electrons fill the available energy levels starting from the shell nearest to the nucleus before occupying higher energy levels.

Basic Rules

• Electrons occupy the lowest energy level first.

• The first shell (K) can hold a maximum of 2 electrons.

• The second shell (L) can hold a maximum of 8 electrons.

• For the first twenty elements, the third shell (M) is filled with a maximum of 8 electrons before the fourth shell (N) begins to fill.

• The total number of electrons in a neutral atom is equal to its atomic number.

Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the electrons found in the outermost energy level (outer shell) of an atom. These electrons take part in chemical bonding and determine the chemical properties of an element.

Importance of Valence Electrons

• Determine the chemical properties of an element.

• Determine the valency of an element.

• Participate in chemical bonding.

• Determine the position of an element in the Periodic Table.

Valency

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom. It is the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares during a chemical reaction in order to attain a stable electronic configuration.

Examples of Valency

Octet Rule

The Octet Rule states that atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons in order to obtain eight electrons in their outermost shell, making them stable like the noble gases.

Exception: Hydrogen and helium become stable with only two electrons (duplet rule).

Relationship Between Electronic Configuration and the Periodic Table

The electronic configuration of an element determines its position in the Periodic Table. Elements with similar electronic configurations have similar chemical properties.

Periods

A period is a horizontal row in the Periodic Table.

The period number of an element is equal to the number of occupied electron shells.

Examples

• Sodium (2,8,1) has three occupied shells, so it is in Period 3.

• Calcium (2,8,8,2) has four occupied shells, so it is in Period 4.

Groups

A group is a vertical column in the Periodic Table.

For the main-group elements, the group number is equal to the number of valence electrons.

Examples

• Lithium (2,1) belongs to Group 1.

• Magnesium (2,8,2) belongs to Group 2.

• Aluminium (2,8,3) belongs to Group 13 (Group III).

• Oxygen (2,6) belongs to Group 16 (Group VI).

• Chlorine (2,8,7) belongs to Group 17 (Group VII).

• Neon (2,8) belongs to Group 18 (Group VIII/0).

Noble Gas Electronic Configuration

The noble gases have completely filled outermost shells, making them chemically stable and generally unreactive.

Element Electronic Configuration Reason for Stability
Helium 2 Complete duplet
Neon 2,8 Complete octet
Argon 2,8,8 Complete octet

Importance of Electronic Configuration

• Determines the chemical properties of an element.

• Helps predict the valency of an element.

• Explains chemical bonding.

• Determines the position of an element in the Periodic Table.

• Explains why some elements are reactive while others are stable.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking electrons are arranged randomly around the nucleus.

✅ Electrons occupy specific energy levels (shells) in a definite order.

❌ Confusing valency with the number of valence electrons.

✅ Valency is the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares to become stable, while valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell.

❌ Believing every shell can hold only eight electrons.

✅ The maximum number of electrons depends on the shell. For example, K holds 2, L holds 8, M holds up to 18 and N holds up to 32 electrons.

❌ Thinking noble gases are highly reactive.

✅ Noble gases are generally unreactive because they have completely filled outermost electron shells.

❌ Confusing periods with groups in the Periodic Table.

✅ Periods are horizontal rows, while groups are vertical columns.

❌ Believing all atoms follow the octet rule.

✅ Hydrogen and helium are stable with two electrons (duplet rule), while most other elements tend to achieve eight electrons in their outermost shell.

⭐ Relate electronic configuration to the group and period of an element.

⭐ Understand the octet rule and the stability of noble gases.

⭐ Practise writing electronic configurations accurately.

Chemical Symbols

A chemical symbol is the one-letter or two-letter abbreviation used to represent an element.

Rules for Writing Chemical Symbols

• The first letter is always written as a capital letter.

• If there is a second letter, it is written as a small letter.

A chemical formula is a symbolic representation of a substance using the chemical symbols of the elements present and the number of atoms of each element.

Types of Chemical Formulae

Meaning of a Chemical Equation

A chemical equation is a symbolic representation of a chemical reaction using chemical symbols and formulae to show the reactants and products.

Parts of a Chemical Equation

Reactants
These are the substances present before a chemical reaction takes place.

Products
These are the new substances formed after the reaction.

Arrow (→)
Shows the direction in which the reaction proceeds from reactants to products.

Writing Chemical Equations

Chemical equations must be written correctly to show the reactants, products and the conservation of atoms during a chemical reaction.

Examples of Chemical Equations

Formation of Water

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Formation of Carbon Dioxide

C + O₂ → CO₂

Formation of Sodium Chloride

2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl

Decomposition of Calcium Carbonate

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Formation of Ammonia (Haber Process)

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Balanced Chemical Equations

A balanced chemical equation has an equal number of atoms of each element on both the reactant and product sides. This follows the Law of Conservation of Mass, which states that matter cannot be created or destroyed during a chemical reaction.

Examples of Balanced Equations

Balanced Equation Reaction

2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl Formation of sodium chloride
C + O₂ → CO₂ Combustion of carbon
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O Combustion of methane

Word Equations

A word equation describes a chemical reaction using the names of substances instead of their chemical symbols.

Examples

Hydrogen + Oxygen → Water

Carbon + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide

Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium Oxide

Sodium + Chlorine → Sodium Chloride

Methane + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water

Types of Chemical Reactions

Chemical reactions can be classified into different types based on how the reactants are converted into products.

1. Combination (Synthesis) Reaction

A combination reaction occurs when two or more substances combine to form a single product.

Example:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

2. Decomposition Reaction

A decomposition reaction occurs when one compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances.

Example:

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

3. Single Displacement Reaction

A single displacement reaction occurs when a more reactive element replaces a less reactive element in a compound.

Example:

Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu

4. Double Displacement Reaction

A double displacement reaction occurs when two compounds exchange ions to form two new compounds.

Example:

AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃

5. Combustion Reaction

A combustion reaction occurs when a substance burns in oxygen, releasing heat and usually light.

Example:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Indicators of a Chemical Reaction

• Change in colour.

• Production of a gas.

• Formation of a precipitate.

• Change in temperature (heat released or absorbed).

• Emission of light.

• Change in smell.

Law of Conservation of Mass

The Law of Conservation of Mass states that matter can neither be created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction. Therefore, the total mass of the reactants is equal to the total mass of the products.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking a chemical symbol and a chemical formula are the same.

✅ A chemical symbol represents one element, while a chemical formula represents a substance made of one or more elements.

❌ Forgetting to balance chemical equations.

✅ Every chemical equation must have the same number of atoms of each element on both sides.

❌ Changing subscripts when balancing equations.

✅ Balance equations by changing only the coefficients, never the subscripts in a chemical formula.

❌ Confusing reactants with products.

✅ Reactants are the starting substances, while products are the substances formed after the reaction.

❌ Thinking every reaction produces heat.

✅ Some reactions release heat (exothermic), while others absorb heat (endothermic).

❌ Believing word equations and chemical equations are identical.

✅ Word equations use names of substances, while chemical equations use symbols and chemical formulae.

⭐ Know the difference between symbols, formulae and equations.

⭐ Practise balancing chemical equations regularly.

🧪 CHAPTER 6: SEPARATION OF MIXTURES

Introduction

Many substances occur naturally as mixtures. A mixture contains two or more substances that are physically combined and can be separated without changing their chemical properties. Chemists use different separation techniques depending on the physical properties of the substances in the mixture.

Meaning of a Mixture

A mixture is a physical combination of two or more substances that are not chemically combined. Each component retains its own properties and can be separated by physical methods.

Characteristics of Mixtures

• The substances are not chemically combined.

• Each component retains its original properties.

• The composition of a mixture is not fixed.

• The components can be separated by physical methods.

• Mixtures may be homogeneous or heterogeneous.

Types of Mixtures

Homogeneous Mixture
A homogeneous mixture has a uniform composition throughout.

Examples: Salt solution, sugar solution and air.

Heterogeneous Mixture
A heterogeneous mixture has a non-uniform composition, and its components can often be seen separately.

Examples: Sand and water, oil and water, granite.

Methods of Separating Mixtures

The method used to separate a mixture depends on the physical properties of its components, such as particle size, solubility, boiling point, density and magnetic properties.

1. Hand Picking

Hand picking is used to separate large solid particles that can easily be seen with the naked eye.

Examples:

• Removing stones from rice.

• Picking rotten fruits from good ones.

2. Sieving

Sieving is used to separate solid particles of different sizes using a sieve.

Examples:

• Separating sand from gravel.

• Separating flour from bran.

3. Winnowing

Winnowing is used to separate lighter particles from heavier particles by using wind or moving air.

Examples:

• Separating husks from grains.

• Separating chaff from rice.

4. Magnetic Separation

Magnetic separation is used to separate magnetic materials from non-magnetic materials using a magnet.

Examples:

• Separating iron filings from sand.

• Removing iron particles from scrap materials.

5. Sedimentation and Decantation

Sedimentation is the process in which heavier insoluble particles settle at the bottom of a liquid after standing for some time.

Decantation is the careful pouring off of the clear liquid without disturbing the sediment.

Example:

Separating muddy water from sand.

6. Filtration

Filtration is the process of separating an insoluble solid from a liquid by passing the mixture through a filter paper. The solid remains on the filter paper as the residue, while the liquid that passes through is called the filtrate.

Examples:

• Separating sand from water.

• Separating chalk from water.

7. Evaporation

Evaporation is used to separate a soluble solid from a solution by heating so that the solvent evaporates, leaving the solid behind.

Examples:

• Obtaining common salt from seawater.

• Recovering sugar from sugar solution.

8. Crystallization

Crystallization is the process of obtaining pure crystals of a soluble substance from its saturated solution by cooling or slow evaporation.

Examples:

• Purification of copper(II) sulfate crystals.

• Preparation of alum crystals.

9. Distillation

Distillation is used to separate a liquid from a solution or to separate two miscible liquids with different boiling points. The liquid is heated until it vaporizes and then cooled to form a pure liquid called the distillate.

Examples:

• Obtaining pure water from salt solution.

• Production of distilled water.

10. Fractional Distillation

Fractional distillation is used to separate two or more miscible liquids that have different boiling points.

Examples:

• Separation of crude oil into useful fractions.

• Separation of ethanol from water.

11. Chromatography

Chromatography is a method used to separate the different coloured substances or components in a mixture based on their different rates of movement through a stationary medium.

Examples:

• Separating the colours in black ink.

• Separating plant pigments such as chlorophyll.

12. Separating Funnel

A separating funnel is used to separate two immiscible liquids (liquids that do not mix) based on their different densities.

Examples:

• Separating oil from water.

• Separating kerosene from water.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking all mixtures can be separated using the same method.

✅ Different mixtures require different separation methods based on their physical properties.

❌ Confusing filtration with evaporation.

✅ Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid, while evaporation separates a soluble solid from its solution.

❌ Thinking distillation and fractional distillation are the same.

✅ Distillation separates a liquid from a solution, while fractional distillation separates two or more miscible liquids with different boiling points.

❌ Believing chromatography is only used in laboratories.

✅ Chromatography is also used in medicine, food testing, forensic science and environmental analysis.

❌ Confusing homogeneous mixtures with heterogeneous mixtures.

✅ Homogeneous mixtures have a uniform composition, while heterogeneous mixtures have a non-uniform composition.

❌ Thinking separation changes the chemical composition of substances.

✅ Separation methods are physical processes and do not produce new substances.

🔥 CHAPTER 10: OXIDATION AND REDUCTION (REDOX REACTIONS)

Introduction

Oxidation and reduction are important chemical processes that occur in many natural and industrial reactions. These reactions are collectively called redox (reduction-oxidation) reactions because oxidation and reduction always occur together.

Meaning of Oxidation

Oxidation is the process in which a substance gains oxygen, loses hydrogen or loses electrons during a chemical reaction.

Examples of Oxidation

• Burning of magnesium in oxygen.

• Rusting of iron.

• Burning of fuels such as wood and petrol.

Meaning of Reduction

Reduction is the process in which a substance loses oxygen, gains hydrogen or gains electrons during a chemical reaction.

Examples of Reduction

• Extraction of iron from iron ore.

• Conversion of copper(II) oxide to copper.

Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

An oxidizing agent is a substance that causes another substance to be oxidized by supplying oxygen or accepting electrons. During the reaction, the oxidizing agent is reduced.

Examples of Oxidizing Agents

• Oxygen (O₂).

• Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄).

• Potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇).

• Chlorine (Cl₂).

A reducing agent is a substance that causes another substance to be reduced by removing oxygen or donating electrons. During the reaction, the reducing agent is oxidized.

Examples of Reducing Agents

• Hydrogen (H₂).

• Carbon (C).

• Carbon monoxide (CO).

• Zinc (Zn).

Examples of Redox Reactions

Example 1

CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O

In this reaction, copper(II) oxide loses oxygen and is reduced to copper, while hydrogen gains oxygen and is oxidized to water.

Example 2

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Magnesium gains oxygen and is oxidized, while oxygen is reduced.

Example 3

Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂

Iron(III) oxide is reduced to iron, while carbon monoxide is oxidized to carbon dioxide.

Rusting of Iron

Rusting is the slow oxidation of iron in the presence of oxygen and water, producing hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).

Conditions Necessary for Rusting

• Presence of oxygen.

• Presence of water (moisture).

Prevention of Rusting

Rusting can be prevented by preventing iron from coming into contact with oxygen and water.

Methods of Preventing Rusting

• Painting iron surfaces.

• Applying grease or oil.

• Galvanizing (coating iron with zinc).

• Electroplating with metals such as chromium or tin.

• Alloying iron to produce stainless steel.

• Keeping iron objects dry.

Applications of Redox Reactions

• Extraction of metals from their ores.

• Manufacture of chemicals.

• Respiration in living organisms.

• Photosynthesis in plants.

• Combustion of fuels.

• Production of electricity in batteries and fuel cells.

• Corrosion and rusting processes.

Differences Between Oxidation and Reduction

Oxidation Reduction
Gain of oxygen. Loss of oxygen.
Loss of hydrogen. Gain of hydrogen.
Loss of electrons. Gain of electrons.
Oxidation number increases. Oxidation number decreases.

Importance of Redox Reactions

• Essential in energy production.

• Used in metallurgy for extracting metals.

• Important in industrial chemical processes.

• Occur during respiration and photosynthesis.

• Used in bleaching and water purification.

• Form the basis of electrochemical cells and batteries.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking oxidation only means adding oxygen.

✅ Oxidation can also mean the loss of electrons or the loss of hydrogen.

❌ Thinking reduction only means removing oxygen.

✅ Reduction can also mean the gain of electrons or the gain of hydrogen.

❌ Confusing oxidizing agents with reducing agents.

✅ An oxidizing agent gains electrons and is reduced, while a reducing agent loses electrons and is oxidized.

❌ Believing oxidation and reduction occur separately.

✅ Oxidation and reduction always occur together in a redox reaction.

❌ Thinking rusting occurs only because of water.

✅ Rusting requires both oxygen and water (moisture).

❌ Assuming all corrosion is rusting.

✅ Rusting is the corrosion of iron and steel, while other metals undergo different forms of corrosion.

⚛️ CHAPTER 12: THE PERIODIC TABLE

Introduction

The Periodic Table is a systematic arrangement of all known chemical elements according to their atomic numbers and recurring chemical properties. It helps scientists predict the properties and behaviour of elements and is one of the most important tools in Chemistry.

History of the Periodic Table

The modern Periodic Table developed through the work of several scientists. In 1869, Dmitri Mendeleev arranged elements according to increasing atomic mass and left spaces for undiscovered elements. The modern table is now arranged according to increasing atomic number, following the work of Henry Moseley.

Modern Periodic Law

The Modern Periodic Law states that:

"The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers."

Arrangement of the Periodic Table

The Periodic Table is arranged into:

• Periods (horizontal rows).

• Groups (vertical columns).

Periods

A period is a horizontal row of elements. There are 7 periods in the modern Periodic Table.

Elements in the same period have:

• The same number of electron shells.

• Increasing atomic numbers from left to right.

Groups

A group is a vertical column of elements. There are 18 groups in the modern Periodic Table.

Elements in the same group have:

• The same number of valence (outermost) electrons.

• Similar chemical properties.

• Similar valency.

Classification of Elements

The elements are broadly classified into:

• Metals

• Non-metals

• Metalloids (Semi-metals)

Metals

Metals are elements that readily lose electrons to form positive ions (cations). Most metals are found on the left and centre of the Periodic Table.

Examples of Metals

• Sodium (Na).

• Magnesium (Mg).

• Aluminium (Al).

• Iron (Fe).

• Copper (Cu).

• Gold (Au).

Properties of Metals

• Good conductors of heat and electricity.

• Lustrous (shiny).

• Malleable (can be hammered into sheets).

• Ductile (can be drawn into wires).

• Usually hard and strong.

• Generally have high melting and boiling points.

• Usually solid at room temperature (except mercury).

Non-metals

Non-metals are elements that usually gain electrons to form negative ions (anions). They are mainly found on the right side of the Periodic Table.

Examples of Non-metals

• Hydrogen (H).

• Carbon (C).

• Nitrogen (N).

• Oxygen (O).

• Sulfur (S).

• Chlorine (Cl).

Properties of Non-metals

• Poor conductors of heat and electricity.

• Usually dull in appearance.

• Brittle when solid.

• Cannot be hammered into sheets.

• Cannot be drawn into wires.

• Many are gases at room temperature.

• Generally have lower melting and boiling points than metals.

Examples of Metalloids

• Boron (B).

• Silicon (Si).

• Germanium (Ge).

• Arsenic (As).

• Antimony (Sb).

• Tellurium (Te).

Uses of Metalloids

Some groups of elements have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons.

Periodic trends are the regular patterns in the physical and chemical properties of elements as you move across a period or down a group in the Periodic Table.

Atomic radius is the distance from the centre of an atom to its outermost electron shell.

Trend Across a Period

• Atomic radius decreases from left to right.

Trend Down a Group

• Atomic radius increases from top to bottom.

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove the outermost electron from an isolated gaseous atom.

Trend Across a Period

• Ionization energy generally increases.

Trend Down a Group

• Ionization energy generally decreases.

Trend Across a Period

Trend Down a Group

Metallic character refers to how easily an element loses electrons to form positive ions.

Trend Across a Period

• Metallic character decreases from left to right.

Trend Down a Group

• Metallic character increases from top to bottom.

5. Chemical Reactivity

Metals

• Reactivity increases down Groups 1 and 2.

Non-metals

• Reactivity decreases down Group 17 (Halogens).

🤝 CHAPTER 13: CHEMICAL BONDING

Introduction

Chemical bonding is the force of attraction that holds atoms or ions together to form molecules and compounds. Atoms bond together to achieve a more stable electronic configuration, usually by attaining a complete outer electron shell.

Why Do Atoms Form Bonds?

Atoms form chemical bonds in order to:

• Achieve a stable electron arrangement.

• Attain the electronic configuration of noble gases.

• Lower their energy and become more stable.

Types of Chemical Bonds

The three major types of chemical bonds are:

• Ionic (Electrovalent) Bond.

• Covalent Bond.

• Metallic Bond.

1. Ionic (Electrovalent) Bond

An ionic bond is formed when one or more electrons are transferred from a metal atom to a non-metal atom, producing positive and negative ions that are held together by electrostatic attraction.

Examples:

• Sodium chloride (NaCl).

• Magnesium oxide (MgO).

• Calcium chloride (CaCl₂).

Formation of Ionic Bonds

In ionic bonding, a metal atom loses one or more electrons to become a positive ion (cation), while a non-metal atom gains those electrons to become a negative ion (anion). The opposite charges attract each other to form an ionic compound.

Example: Formation of Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

Step 1: Sodium loses one electron.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Step 2: Chlorine gains one electron.

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Step 3: The ions attract each other.

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl

Properties of Ionic Compounds

• High melting and boiling points.

• Usually hard and brittle.

• Soluble in water.

• Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water.

• Usually exist as crystalline solids.

2. Covalent Bond

A covalent bond is formed when two non-metal atoms share one or more pairs of electrons in order to achieve stable electron configurations.

Examples:

• Hydrogen (H₂).

• Oxygen (O₂).

• Water (H₂O).

• Carbon dioxide (CO₂).

• Methane (CH₄).

Formation of a Covalent Bond

Example: Hydrogen Molecule (H₂)

Each hydrogen atom contributes one electron, forming a shared pair of electrons.

H + H → H:H (H₂)

Properties of Covalent Compounds

• Usually have low melting and boiling points.

• Poor conductors of electricity.

• May exist as gases, liquids or soft solids.

• Generally insoluble in water.

• Usually soluble in organic solvents.

3. Metallic Bond

A metallic bond is the force of attraction between positive metal ions and a "sea" of delocalized (free-moving) electrons in a metal.

Formation of Metallic Bonds

Metal atoms lose their outermost electrons, which become free to move throughout the metal. These free electrons hold the positive metal ions together, forming a strong metallic bond.

Properties of Metals Due to Metallic Bonding

• Good conductors of electricity.

• Good conductors of heat.

• Malleable (can be hammered into sheets).

• Ductile (can be drawn into wires).

• Strong and durable.

• High melting and boiling points.

Comparison of Ionic, Covalent and Metallic Bonds

Property Ionic Bond Covalent Bond Metallic Bond
Particles Involved Metal + Non-metal Non-metal + Non-metal Metal atoms
Formation Electron transfer Electron sharing Delocalized electrons
Electrical Conductivity Conducts when molten or in solution Poor conductor Excellent conductor
Melting Point Usually high Usually low Usually high
Examples NaCl, MgO H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ Iron, Copper, Aluminium

Valency

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom. It is the number of electrons an atom loses, gains or shares when forming chemical bonds.

Examples of Common Valencies

• Hydrogen = 1

• Oxygen = 2

• Nitrogen = 3

• Carbon = 4

• Sodium = 1

• Magnesium = 2

• Aluminium = 3

• Chlorine = 1

Factors Affecting Chemical Bond Formation

Several factors determine how atoms bond together and the type of bond they form.

1. Number of Valence Electrons

Atoms with nearly empty or nearly full outer electron shells tend to form bonds to achieve a stable electronic configuration.

2. Electronegativity Difference

The difference in electronegativity between two atoms determines the type of bond formed.

• Large difference → Ionic bond.

• Small difference → Covalent bond.

3. Atomic Size

The size of atoms affects the strength and length of chemical bonds.

4. Number of Electrons Shared or Transferred

Atoms may transfer or share one or more electrons depending on their electronic configurations.

Applications of Chemical Bonding

• Manufacture of medicines.

• Production of plastics and polymers.

• Construction materials such as steel and cement.

• Electronics and semiconductor industries.

• Food processing and preservation.

• Production of fertilizers.

Importance of Chemical Bonding

• Explains how compounds are formed.

• Helps predict the properties of substances.

• Explains chemical reactions.

• Essential in medicine, engineering and industry.

• Forms the basis of materials science and nanotechnology.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking ionic bonds involve the sharing of electrons.

✅ Ionic bonds are formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal.

❌ Thinking covalent bonds involve electron transfer.

✅ Covalent bonds are formed by the sharing of electrons between non-metal atoms.

❌ Confusing ionic compounds with covalent compounds.

✅ Ionic compounds usually have high melting points and conduct electricity when molten or dissolved, while covalent compounds generally have lower melting points and do not conduct electricity.

❌ Believing metals conduct electricity because their atoms move freely.

✅ Metals conduct electricity because they contain delocalized (free-moving) electrons.

❌ Assuming all covalent compounds are gases.

✅ Covalent compounds can exist as gases, liquids or solids depending on their structure.

❌ Confusing valency with atomic number.

✅ Valency is the combining capacity of an atom, while atomic number is the number of protons in an atom.

🌡️ CHAPTER 14: STATES OF MATTER

Introduction

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Matter exists mainly in three physical states: solid, liquid and gas. Under special conditions, matter can also exist as plasma and Bose-Einstein condensate.

The Three Main States of Matter

1. Solid

In solids, particles are closely packed together and vibrate about fixed positions.

Properties of Solids

• Definite shape.

• Definite volume.

• High density.

• Particles are tightly packed.

• Very difficult to compress.

• Strong forces of attraction between particles.

2. Liquid

In liquids, particles are close together but can move freely past one another. This allows liquids to flow while maintaining a definite volume.

Properties of Liquids

• No definite shape (take the shape of their container).

• Definite volume.

• Flow easily.

• Slightly compressible.

• Moderate forces of attraction between particles.

• Moderate particle movement.

3. Gas

In gases, particles are far apart and move rapidly in all directions. The forces of attraction between particles are very weak.

Properties of Gases

• No definite shape.

• No definite volume.

• Expand to fill any container.

• Highly compressible.

• Very low density.

• Particles move freely and rapidly.

Comparison of the Three States of Matter

Property Solid Liquid Gas
Shape Definite Takes container's shape No definite shape
Volume Definite Definite No definite volume
Particle Arrangement Closely packed Close together Far apart
Compressibility Very low Low High
Particle Movement Vibrate only Slide past each other Move freely

Kinetic Theory of Matter

The kinetic theory of matter explains the behaviour of solids, liquids and gases based on the movement of particles.

Main Postulates

• Matter is made up of tiny particles.

• The particles are in constant random motion.

• There are spaces between particles.

• Particles attract one another.

• Heating increases the kinetic energy and speed of particles.

Changes of State

A change of state is the physical process by which matter changes from one state to another due to the gain or loss of heat energy.

1. Melting (Fusion)

Melting is the change of a substance from a solid to a liquid when heat is supplied.

Example: Ice → Water.

2. Freezing (Solidification)

Freezing is the change of a substance from a liquid to a solid when heat is removed.

Example: Water → Ice.

3. Evaporation

Evaporation is the slow change of a liquid into a gas from its surface at temperatures below its boiling point.

Examples:

• Drying of wet clothes.

• Drying of puddles after rain.

4. Boiling

Boiling is the rapid conversion of a liquid into a gas throughout the liquid when it reaches its boiling point.

Example: Water boils at 100°C under standard atmospheric pressure.

5. Condensation

Condensation is the change of a gas into a liquid when heat is removed.

Examples:

• Formation of dew.

• Water droplets on a cold bottle.

6. Sublimation

Sublimation is the direct change of a solid into a gas without passing through the liquid state.

Examples:

• Iodine.

• Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide).

• Naphthalene (mothballs).

7. Deposition

Deposition is the direct change of a gas into a solid without passing through the liquid state.

Example: Formation of frost on cold surfaces.

Heating and Cooling Curves

Heating and cooling curves show how the temperature of a substance changes as heat is added or removed. During melting and boiling, the temperature remains constant until the change of state is complete.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration until they are evenly distributed.

Examples of Diffusion

• The smell of perfume spreading through a room.

• A drop of ink spreading in water.

• Exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide in the lungs.

• The smell of cooking spreading around a house.

Factors Affecting the Rate of Diffusion

• Temperature.

• Size (mass) of the particles.

• Concentration difference.

• Nature (density) of the medium.

Brownian Motion

Brownian motion is the continuous random movement of tiny particles suspended in a liquid or gas due to constant collisions with the molecules of the fluid.

Importance of Brownian Motion

• Provides evidence that matter is made up of tiny moving particles.

• Supports the kinetic theory of matter.

Plasma (The Fourth State of Matter)

Plasma is an ionized state of matter consisting of positively charged ions and free electrons. It is formed when a gas is heated to a very high temperature or subjected to a strong electric field.

Examples of Plasma

• The Sun and other stars.

• Lightning.

• Fluorescent lamps.

• Neon signs.

Applications of States of Matter

• Manufacture of medicines.

• Refrigeration and air conditioning.

• Food preservation.

• Industrial manufacturing processes.

• Weather and climate studies.

• Medical and laboratory applications.

Importance of States of Matter

• Explains the behaviour of solids, liquids and gases.

• Helps us understand physical and chemical changes.

• Essential in engineering and industrial processes.

• Important in environmental and atmospheric studies.

• Forms the foundation for advanced Chemistry concepts.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking particles in solids do not move.

✅ Particles in solids are in constant motion but only vibrate about their fixed positions.

❌ Confusing evaporation with boiling.

✅ Evaporation occurs only at the surface of a liquid and can happen at any temperature, while boiling occurs throughout the liquid at its boiling point.

❌ Believing gases have no mass.

✅ Gases have mass and occupy space just like solids and liquids.

❌ Thinking melting and dissolving are the same process.

✅ Melting is the change of a solid into a liquid by heating, while dissolving is the mixing of a substance with a solvent to form a solution.

❌ Assuming diffusion occurs only in gases.

✅ Diffusion occurs in solids, liquids and gases, although it is fastest in gases.

❌ Thinking plasma is the same as a gas.

✅ Plasma is an ionized state of matter containing free electrons and ions, making it different from ordinary gases.

🧪 CHAPTER 15: MOLE CONCEPT AND STOICHIOMETRY

Introduction

The mole concept is one of the most important ideas in Chemistry. It provides a way of counting extremely small particles such as atoms, molecules and ions and is used in calculating the amounts of substances involved in chemical reactions. Stoichiometry is the calculation of the quantities of reactants and products in chemical reactions.

Meaning of a Mole

A mole (mol) is the amount of substance that contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons). This number is known as Avogadro's constant or Avogadro's number.

Importance of the Mole Concept

• Enables chemists to count tiny particles.

• Helps calculate the masses of substances in reactions.

• Used in preparing chemical solutions.

• Essential in laboratory calculations.

• Forms the basis of stoichiometric calculations.

Molar Mass

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance. It is expressed in grams per mole (g/mol).

Examples

• Hydrogen (H₂) = 2 g/mol.

• Oxygen (O₂) = 32 g/mol.

• Water (H₂O) = 18 g/mol.

• Carbon dioxide (CO₂) = 44 g/mol.

Relationship Between Mass, Moles and Molar Mass

The number of moles of a substance can be calculated using the formula:

Number of Moles = Mass ÷ Molar Mass

Rearranging the formula:

Mass = Number of Moles × Molar Mass

Molar Mass = Mass ÷ Number of Moles

Avogadro's Constant

Avogadro's constant is 6.022 × 10²³ particles per mole. It represents the number of particles present in one mole of any substance.

Relationship Between Moles and Number of Particles

Number of Particles = Number of Moles × 6.022 × 10²³

Number of Moles = Number of Particles ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Molar Volume of Gases

At Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP), one mole of any gas occupies a volume of 22.4 dm³ (22.4 L).

Formula

Number of Moles = Gas Volume ÷ 22.4 dm³

Gas Volume = Number of Moles × 22.4 dm³

Empirical Formula

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.

Example

Hydrogen peroxide has the molecular formula H₂O₂, but its empirical formula is HO.

Molecular Formula

The molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms of each element present in one molecule of a compound.

Example

Empirical Formula: CH₂

Molecular Formula: C₂H₄

Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry is the branch of Chemistry that deals with the quantitative relationship between reactants and products in a balanced chemical equation.

Balanced Chemical Equations

A balanced chemical equation contains equal numbers of atoms of each element on both sides of the equation, in accordance with the Law of Conservation of Mass.

Example:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Importance of Balanced Equations

• Show the correct proportions of reactants and products.

• Help in mole calculations.

• Help determine the amount of substances required or produced in reactions.

• Support industrial chemical production.

Limiting Reactant

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is completely used up first in a chemical reaction. It determines the maximum amount of product that can be formed.

Percentage Yield

Percentage yield measures the efficiency of a chemical reaction.

Formula

Percentage Yield = (Actual Yield ÷ Theoretical Yield) × 100%

Percentage Purity

Percentage purity indicates how much of a sample is the desired pure substance.

Formula

Percentage Purity = (Mass of Pure Substance ÷ Total Mass of Sample) × 100%

Applications of Stoichiometry

• Manufacturing industries.

• Pharmaceutical production.

• Food processing industries.

• Laboratory experiments.

• Agriculture (fertilizer production).

• Environmental pollution control.

Importance of Stoichiometry

• Minimizes waste during chemical production.

• Reduces production costs.

• Improves industrial efficiency.

• Helps predict reaction products accurately.

• Essential for scientific research and chemical engineering.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking one mole means one gram.

✅ One mole is an amount of substance containing 6.022 × 10²³ particles. Its mass depends on the substance's molar mass.

❌ Confusing molar mass with molecular mass.

✅ Molar mass is expressed in g/mol, while molecular mass is the relative mass of a molecule compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

❌ Using an unbalanced chemical equation for calculations.

✅ Always balance a chemical equation before performing stoichiometric calculations.

❌ Thinking the reactant with the greatest mass is always the limiting reactant.

✅ The limiting reactant depends on the number of moles, not simply the mass.

❌ Confusing empirical formula with molecular formula.

✅ The empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms, while the molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms in a molecule.

❌ Assuming percentage yield is always 100%.

✅ In practice, percentage yield is often less than 100% because of side reactions, impurities and product loss during collection.

Exam Tips

⭐ Define mole, molar mass, Avogadro's constant and stoichiometry correctly.

⭐ Memorize Avogadro's constant (6.022 × 10²³).

⭐ Know the relationship between mass, moles and molar mass.

⭐ Use only balanced chemical equations in calculations.

⭐ Distinguish between empirical and molecular formulae.

⭐ Understand limiting reactants, percentage yield and percentage purity.

⭐ Examiners frequently ask students to calculate moles, masses, gas volumes, empirical formulae and percentage yield.

🔥 CHAPTER 16: ENERGY CHANGES (THERMOCHEMISTRY)

Introduction

Thermochemistry is the branch of Chemistry that studies the heat energy absorbed or released during physical and chemical changes. Almost every chemical reaction involves an energy change.

Forms of Energy

The common forms of energy involved in chemical reactions include:

• Heat energy.

• Light energy.

• Electrical energy.

• Chemical energy.

• Nuclear energy.

Exothermic Reactions

An exothermic reaction is a chemical reaction that releases heat energy to the surroundings.

Characteristics of Exothermic Reactions

• Heat is given out.

• The surroundings become warmer.

• Products have lower energy than the reactants.

Examples:

• Combustion of fuels.

• Neutralization of an acid with a base.

• Respiration.

Endothermic Reactions

An endothermic reaction is a chemical reaction that absorbs heat energy from the surroundings.

Characteristics of Endothermic Reactions

• Heat is absorbed.

• The surroundings become cooler.

• Products have higher energy than the reactants.

Examples:

• Photosynthesis.

• Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate.

• Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water.

Enthalpy Change (ΔH)

Enthalpy change (ΔH) is the heat energy change that occurs during a chemical reaction at constant pressure.

Types of Enthalpy Changes

• Enthalpy of combustion.

• Enthalpy of neutralization.

• Enthalpy of formation.

• Enthalpy of solution.

Energy Profile Diagram

An energy profile diagram shows the energy changes that occur during a chemical reaction from reactants to products.

For an exothermic reaction:

• Reactants have higher energy than products.

• ΔH is negative.

For an endothermic reaction:

• Products have higher energy than reactants.

• ΔH is positive.

Activation Energy

Activation energy is the minimum amount of energy required for a chemical reaction to begin.

Even exothermic reactions require activation energy before they can proceed.

Catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being permanently changed or consumed.

How Catalysts Work

• They provide an alternative reaction pathway.

• They lower the activation energy.

• They increase the reaction rate.

Examples of Catalysts

• Iron in the Haber process.

• Vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process.

• Manganese(IV) oxide in the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.

• Enzymes in living organisms.

Applications of Thermochemistry

• Manufacture of chemicals.

• Production of electricity in power stations.

• Fuel combustion in vehicles and industries.

• Food production and preservation.

• Refrigeration and air conditioning.

• Medical and pharmaceutical industries.

Importance of Thermochemistry

• Helps determine the energy required for chemical reactions.

• Improves industrial efficiency.

• Reduces energy wastage.

• Supports the design of industrial processes.

• Helps explain natural and biological processes.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking exothermic reactions absorb heat.

✅ Exothermic reactions release heat to the surroundings.

❌ Thinking endothermic reactions release heat.

✅ Endothermic reactions absorb heat from the surroundings.

❌ Assuming catalysts increase the amount of products formed.

✅ Catalysts only increase the rate of reaction; they do not change the final amount of products.

❌ Believing catalysts are used up during reactions.

✅ Catalysts remain chemically unchanged after the reaction.

❌ Confusing activation energy with enthalpy change.

✅ Activation energy is the minimum energy needed to start a reaction, while enthalpy change (ΔH) is the overall heat energy change during the reaction.

Exam Tips

⭐ Distinguish between exothermic and endothermic reactions.

⭐ Know the meaning of activation energy and enthalpy change.

⭐ Understand how catalysts work.

⭐ Interpret simple energy profile diagrams.

⭐ State examples and applications of thermochemistry.

⭐ Examiners commonly ask students to compare exothermic and endothermic reactions, explain catalyst functions and interpret energy diagrams.

⚡ CHAPTER 17: RATES OF CHEMICAL REACTIONS

Introduction

The rate of a chemical reaction is the speed at which reactants are converted into products. Some reactions occur very quickly, while others take a long time.

Collision Theory

The collision theory explains that chemical reactions occur when reacting particles collide with sufficient energy and in the correct orientation. Only effective collisions produce a chemical reaction.

Factors Affecting the Rate of Chemical Reactions

1. Temperature

Increasing the temperature increases the kinetic energy of particles, causing more frequent and energetic collisions. As a result, the reaction rate increases.

2. Concentration

Increasing the concentration of reactants increases the number of particles in a given volume, leading to more frequent collisions and a faster reaction rate.

3. Pressure (For Gases)

Increasing the pressure of reacting gases forces the particles closer together, increasing the frequency of collisions and speeding up the reaction.

4. Surface Area

Breaking a solid into smaller pieces increases its surface area exposed to the reactants, allowing more collisions to occur and increasing the reaction rate.

5. Catalyst

A catalyst increases the rate of a chemical reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy.

6. Nature of Reactants

Some substances react naturally faster than others because of differences in their chemical properties and bond strengths.

Measuring the Rate of Reaction

The rate of a reaction can be measured by observing:

• The volume of gas produced.

• The decrease in mass of the reaction mixture.

• The change in colour.

• The formation of a precipitate.

• The change in concentration of reactants or products over time.

Examples of Fast and Slow Reactions

Fast Reactions

• Burning of petrol.

• Explosion of fireworks.

• Neutralization of acids and bases.

Slow Reactions

• Rusting of iron.

• Fermentation.

• Ripening of fruits.

Applications of Reaction Rates

• Manufacture of fertilizers.

• Food preservation.

• Production of medicines.

• Petroleum refining.

• Pollution control.

• Industrial chemical production.

Importance of Studying Reaction Rates

• Helps improve industrial production.

• Reduces manufacturing costs.

• Increases product quality.

• Improves laboratory experiments.

• Helps control harmful chemical reactions.

• Supports scientific research and technological development.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking all particle collisions produce a reaction.

✅ Only effective collisions with enough energy and proper orientation lead to a chemical reaction.

❌ Believing catalysts are consumed during reactions.

✅ Catalysts remain chemically unchanged after the reaction.

❌ Assuming increasing temperature changes the products formed.

✅ Increasing temperature usually changes the rate of reaction, not the products.

❌ Confusing concentration with pressure.

✅ Concentration mainly affects reactions in solutions, while pressure mainly affects reactions involving gases.

❌ Thinking powdered solids react at the same rate as large lumps.

✅ Powdered solids react faster because they have a larger surface area.

Exam Tips

⭐ Define the rate of reaction and collision theory correctly.

⭐ Explain how temperature, concentration, pressure, surface area and catalysts affect reaction rates.

⭐ Distinguish between fast and slow reactions with examples.

⭐ Know methods of measuring reaction rates.

⭐ State the applications of reaction rates in industry and everyday life.

⭐ Examiners frequently ask students to explain the factors affecting reaction rates, apply collision theory and interpret simple reaction-rate graphs.

⚖️ CHAPTER 18: CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM

Introduction

Chemical equilibrium is the dynamic state in a reversible reaction where the rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate of the reverse reaction. At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant.

Reversible Reactions

A reversible reaction is a reaction that can proceed in both the forward and reverse directions under suitable conditions.

Example:

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)

This reaction is used in the Haber Process for the manufacture of ammonia.

Characteristics of Chemical Equilibrium

• Equilibrium occurs only in a closed system.

• The forward and reverse reactions continue simultaneously.

• The rates of the forward and reverse reactions are equal.

• The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant.

• The reaction appears to have stopped, but it is still occurring.

Le Chatelier's Principle

Le Chatelier's Principle states that when a system at equilibrium is disturbed by a change in concentration, temperature or pressure, the equilibrium shifts in a direction that opposes the change.

Factors Affecting Chemical Equilibrium

1. Change in Concentration

• Increasing the concentration of a reactant shifts the equilibrium toward the products.

• Increasing the concentration of a product shifts the equilibrium toward the reactants.

2. Change in Temperature

• Increasing the temperature favors the endothermic direction.

• Decreasing the temperature favors the exothermic direction.

3. Change in Pressure

• Increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas.

• Decreasing pressure shifts the equilibrium toward the side with more moles of gas.

4. Catalyst

A catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium. It only helps the system reach equilibrium more quickly by increasing the rates of both the forward and reverse reactions equally.

Applications of Chemical Equilibrium

• Manufacture of ammonia (Haber Process).

• Manufacture of sulfuric acid (Contact Process).

• Production of methanol.

• Industrial chemical manufacturing.

• Biological processes in living organisms.

Importance of Chemical Equilibrium

• Increases industrial efficiency.

• Improves product yield.

• Reduces production costs.

• Helps optimize industrial conditions.

• Supports environmental and biological processes.

Equilibrium Constant (K)

The equilibrium constant (K) is a value that expresses the relationship between the concentrations of products and reactants at equilibrium for a reversible reaction at a given temperature.

For a general reaction:

aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD

The equilibrium constant is expressed as:

K = ([C]c × [D]d) ÷ ([A]a × [B]b)

Where:

• [A], [B], [C] and [D] represent the equilibrium concentrations.

• a, b, c and d are the coefficients in the balanced chemical equation.

Interpretation of the Equilibrium Constant

• K > 1: Products are favored at equilibrium.

• K < 1: Reactants are favored at equilibrium.

• K = 1: Reactants and products are present in comparable amounts.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking equilibrium means the reaction has stopped.

✅ At equilibrium, both the forward and reverse reactions continue at the same rate.

❌ Assuming the concentrations of reactants and products are always equal.

✅ Their concentrations remain constant, but they are not necessarily equal.

❌ Believing a catalyst changes the equilibrium position.

✅ A catalyst only speeds up the attainment of equilibrium; it does not shift the equilibrium position.

❌ Thinking equilibrium can occur in an open system.

✅ Chemical equilibrium is established only in a closed system.

❌ Confusing the rate of reaction with the amount of substances present.

✅ At equilibrium, the rates are equal, not necessarily the amounts of reactants and products.

Exam Tips

⭐ Define chemical equilibrium and reversible reactions correctly.

⭐ State Le Chatelier's Principle accurately.

⭐ Explain how changes in concentration, temperature and pressure affect equilibrium.

⭐ Understand the role of catalysts in equilibrium.

⭐ Interpret the value of the equilibrium constant (K).

⭐ Examiners frequently ask students to predict the direction of equilibrium shifts and explain industrial applications such as the Haber and Contact processes.

⚡ CHAPTER 19: ELECTROLYSIS

Introduction

Electrolysis is the chemical decomposition of an ionic compound (electrolyte) in its molten state or aqueous solution by the passage of an electric current.

Basic Terms in Electrolysis

Electrolyte – A substance that conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water because it contains free-moving ions.

Electrode – A conductor through which electric current enters or leaves the electrolyte.

Anode – The positive electrode where oxidation occurs.

Cathode – The negative electrode where reduction occurs.

Cation – A positively charged ion that moves to the cathode.

Anion – A negatively charged ion that moves to the anode.

Process of Electrolysis

When an electric current passes through a molten or aqueous electrolyte, positive ions (cations) move towards the cathode, while negative ions (anions) move towards the anode. Chemical reactions then occur at the electrodes.

Reactions at the Electrodes

At the Cathode (Reduction)

Positive ions gain electrons and are reduced.

Example:

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

At the Anode (Oxidation)

Negative ions lose electrons and are oxidized.

Example:

2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

Electrolysis of Molten Sodium Chloride

Electrolyte: Molten sodium chloride (NaCl)

At the Cathode:

Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na

At the Anode:

2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

Products:

• Sodium metal.

• Chlorine gas.

Electrolysis of Water

Water can be decomposed into hydrogen and oxygen gases by electrolysis.

Products:

• Hydrogen gas is produced at the cathode.

• Oxygen gas is produced at the anode.

Overall Equation:

2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)

Uses of Electrolysis

• Electroplating metals.

• Extraction of reactive metals such as aluminium.

• Purification (electrorefining) of copper.

• Manufacture of chlorine, hydrogen and sodium hydroxide.

• Production of oxygen and hydrogen gases.

Electroplating

Electroplating is the process of coating one metal with a thin layer of another metal using electrolysis.

Examples:

• Silver-plating jewellery.

• Chromium-plating car parts.

• Gold-plating electronic components.

Electrorefining (Purification of Metals)

Electrorefining is the process of purifying impure metals by electrolysis. It is commonly used to obtain high-purity metals for industrial and electrical applications.

Example: Purification of copper.

Applications of Electrolysis

• Extraction of aluminium from bauxite.

• Purification of copper and other metals.

• Electroplating jewellery and household items.

• Manufacture of chlorine, hydrogen and sodium hydroxide.

• Production of oxygen for hospitals and industries.

• Manufacture of rechargeable batteries and electronic devices.

Importance of Electrolysis

• Produces pure metals.

• Protects metals from corrosion through electroplating.

• Supports chemical manufacturing industries.

• Provides important industrial gases.

• Plays a major role in modern technology and engineering.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking electrolysis can occur in solid ionic compounds.

✅ Electrolysis occurs only when the electrolyte is molten or dissolved in water, allowing ions to move freely.

❌ Confusing the anode with the cathode.

✅ Anode: Oxidation occurs. Cathode: Reduction occurs.

❌ Believing electrons move through the electrolyte.

✅ Electric current in the electrolyte is carried by ions, while electrons move through the external circuit.

❌ Assuming electroplating and electrorefining are the same.

✅ Electroplating coats a metal with another metal, while electrorefining purifies an impure metal.

Exam Tips

⭐ Define electrolysis, electrolyte, anode and cathode correctly.

⭐ Distinguish between oxidation and reduction at the electrodes.

⭐ Write electrode equations for simple electrolysis reactions.

⭐ Explain the electrolysis of molten sodium chloride and water.

⭐ State the uses of electrolysis, electroplating and electrorefining.

⭐ Examiners frequently ask students to identify products at the electrodes, write half-equations and explain industrial applications of electrolysis.

☢️ CHAPTER 20: NUCLEAR CHEMISTRY (RADIOACTIVITY)

Introduction

Nuclear Chemistry is the branch of Chemistry that studies the structure, changes and reactions of atomic nuclei. One of the most important topics in nuclear chemistry is radioactivity, which involves the spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable atomic nuclei.

Radioactivity

Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei accompanied by the emission of radiation in the form of alpha (α), beta (β) and gamma (γ) rays.

Radioactive Elements

Radioactive elements have unstable nuclei and naturally emit radiation to become more stable.

Examples of Radioactive Elements

• Uranium (U).

• Thorium (Th).

• Radium (Ra).

• Radon (Rn).

• Polonium (Po).

Types of Radioactive Emissions

1. Alpha (α) Particles

Alpha particles consist of two protons and two neutrons. They are identical to the nucleus of a helium atom.

Properties

• Positively charged (+2).

• Heavy and relatively slow.

• Low penetrating power.

• Can be stopped by a sheet of paper or human skin.

2. Beta (β) Particles

Beta particles are high-speed electrons or positrons emitted from unstable nuclei.

Properties

• Negatively charged (β⁻) or positively charged (β⁺).

• Lighter than alpha particles.

• Moderate penetrating power.

• Can be stopped by a thin sheet of aluminium.

3. Gamma (γ) Rays

Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic waves emitted from radioactive nuclei.

Properties

• No mass.

• No electric charge.

• Travel at the speed of light.

• Very high penetrating power.

• Can only be reduced significantly by thick lead or concrete shielding.

Background Radiation

Background radiation is the low level of ionizing radiation that is naturally present in the environment.

Sources of Background Radiation

• Cosmic rays from space.

• Radioactive rocks and soil.

• Radon gas.

• Medical X-rays and other medical procedures.

Half-Life

Half-life is the time required for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay into more stable atoms.

Importance of Half-Life

• Determines the age of archaeological and geological materials.

• Helps estimate how long radioactive waste remains hazardous.

• Guides the safe use of radioactive materials in medicine and industry.

Applications of Radioactivity

Medicine

• Diagnosis of diseases using radioactive tracers.

• Treatment of cancer through radiotherapy.

• Sterilization of medical equipment.

Agriculture

• Preservation of food by irradiation.

• Control of insect pests.

• Improvement of crop varieties.

Industry

• Detecting leaks in pipelines.

• Measuring the thickness of paper and metal sheets.

• Inspecting welds and manufactured products.

Scientific Research

• Carbon-14 dating of fossils and archaeological remains.

• Studying chemical and biological processes using radioactive tracers.

Hazards of Radioactivity

• Damage to body cells and tissues.

• Radiation burns.

• Cancer.

• Genetic mutations.

• Environmental contamination.

Safety Precautions When Handling Radioactive Materials

• Wear protective clothing.

• Minimize exposure time.

• Use thick lead or concrete shielding.

• Handle radioactive materials with remote tools.

• Store radioactive substances in properly shielded containers.

• Dispose of radioactive waste safely according to regulations.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

❌ Thinking all radiation is harmful in every situation.

✅ Radioactive radiation is extremely useful in medicine, agriculture, industry and scientific research when used safely and under proper control.

❌ Confusing alpha, beta and gamma radiation.

✅ Alpha particles have the lowest penetrating power, beta particles have moderate penetrating power, while gamma rays have the highest penetrating power.

❌ Thinking gamma rays have mass and electric charge.

✅ Gamma rays are electromagnetic waves with no mass and no electric charge.

❌ Believing half-life means all radioactive atoms disappear after one half-life.

✅ After one half-life, only half of the radioactive atoms have decayed.

❌ Assuming radioactive waste can be disposed of like ordinary waste.

✅ Radioactive waste requires special handling, storage and disposal to protect people and the environment.

Exam Tips

⭐ Define radioactivity and half-life correctly.

⭐ Compare alpha, beta and gamma radiation.

⭐ State the sources of background radiation.

⭐ Explain the applications of radioactivity in medicine, agriculture, industry and research.

⭐ Describe the hazards of radioactive materials and appropriate safety precautions.

⭐ Examiners frequently ask students to compare radioactive emissions, calculate half-life and explain practical applications of radioactivity.
